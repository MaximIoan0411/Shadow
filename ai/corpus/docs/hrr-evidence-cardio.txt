V. PRD RISK TITLE: RISK OF CARDIOVASCULAR ADAPTATIONS CONTRIBUTING TO 
ADVERSE MISSION PERFORMANCE AND HEALTH OUTCOMES 
Description: Given that exposure to the spaceflight environment can contribute to 
cardiovascular deconditioning, dysfunction, and remodeling, there is a possibility that crews will 
experience impaired performance and negative cardiovascular health outcomes during and 
after spaceflight and planetary operations. 
VI. STATUS 
• Active: Work/research is currently being done towards this risk. 
VII. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
The Risk of Cardiovascular Adaptations Contributing to Adverse Mission Performance and 
Health Outcomes is the integration of three previous risks defined by the National Aeronautics 
and Space Administration’s (NASA) Human System Risk Board (HSRB): Risk of Orthostatic 
Intolerance During Re-Exposure to Gravity, Risk of Cardiac Rhythm Problems During Spaceflight, 
and Risk of Cardiovascular Disease and Other Degenerative Tissue Effects from Radiation 
Exposure. This integrated cardiovascular risk was officially accepted by the Human Systems Risk 
Board (HSRB) in 2021, and the Human Research Program (HRP) is aligning research priorities 
accordingly. 
Manifestations of the cardiovascular risk vary depending upon the Design Reference 
Mission (DRM) category, specifically the length of mission and the distance from Earth. 
Development of dysrhythmias and venous thromboembolism (VTE), which has been reported 
recently, are the primary concerns during a spaceflight mission. However, while there have been 
anecdotal reports and some documented cases of cardiac dysrhythmias during and after 
spaceflight, it is inconclusive whether spaceflight exposure causes or increases the frequency of 
these. An incidental finding of VTE was reported, and NASA has initiated a targeted ultrasound 
surveillance protocol in International Space Station (ISS) crewmembers. Orthostatic intolerance 
after short- and long-duration spaceflight in low Earth orbit (LEO) is well-documented and is 
considered the primary risk during re-exposure to acceleration, including re-entry and landing on 
Earth. Orthostatic intolerance also may be a concern when astronauts are exposed to head-tofoot accelerations during descent to and ascent from the Moon and Mars. Cardiovascular disease 
(CVD) is considered the primary long-term health risk and concern for flight certification, 
especially for long-duration missions beyond LEO when exposure to space radiation will be 
greatest. A recent analysis of astronaut health records demonstrated that the risk of CVD death 
in astronauts is not greater than that of a well-matched cohort, but there is a statistically 
significant greater lifetime risk of a cardiovascular event. Current terrestrial data suggest that the 
lifetime risk of CVD is statistically greater than with normal aging for exposures above 0.5 Gy-Eq 
(e.g., Mars planetary DRM). However, risks to the cardiovascular system from space radiation 
exposure below 0.5 Gy-Eq are not well understood. The interaction between weightlessness or 
altered gravity and prolonged exposures to space radiation beyond LEO also are not yet known. 

This evidence report will address the current state of knowledge in the arena of acute 
and chronic cardiovascular adaptations to spaceflight and in the context of mission phase (in- 
and post-mission), mission duration (weeks, months, and years) and mission characteristics 
(gravity transitions, altered gravity levels, and radiation exposure). This document is not 
intended to be inclusive of all cardiovascular findings in astronauts during and after spaceflight 
and in test subjects participating in spaceflight analogs but will address published scientific 
literature specific to the currently defined performance and health risks. Evidence reports 
evolve and expand based upon new findings as they become available. 
VIII. INTRODUCTION 
Among the concerns of early spaceflight mission planners and medical experts was how 
well the human body would tolerate launch and landing of the space vehicle and whether the 
cardiovascular system would effectively function during high accelerations and in weightlessness. 
Early spaceflight objectives were directed towards launching humans successfully into space and 
safely returning them home. Critical to these objectives were measurements of cardiovascular 
structure and function before, during, and soon after landing to characterize the effects of gravity 
transitions and adaptations to the spaceflight environment in preparation for increasingly longer 
missions in lower Earth orbit (LEO) and later to the Moon. Clinical outcomes and physiological 
assessments were the cornerstone of knowledge during the early spaceflight programs, which 
when combined with later targeted research in weightlessness and spaceflight analogs as well as 
terrestrial medicine yielded the foundation of knowledge in cardiovascular adaptation to 
spaceflight. Our knowledge base has expanded further as more sophisticated medical technology 
has become available, particularly ultrasound, and has been deployed in the spaceflight 
environment. More recently, medical specialists and research teams have turned their attention 
towards integrating this information with investigations regarding the radiation exposures in 
humans and animals to predict mission and health risks during extended duration missions 
beyond LEO and the long-term-health consequences of these spaceflight exposures. 
Cardiovascular function responds acutely to the spaceflight environment (e.g., cardiac chamber 
distension in response to the headward fluid shift) and adapts to the chronic exposure (e.g., 
cardiac atrophy in response to reduced plasma volume and cardiac work), contributing to 
functional outcomes (e.g., orthostatic intolerance and decreased exercise capacity) that affect 
mission critical tasks (e.g., egress), but less is known about the specific mechanisms of adaptation 
and the health effects across the lifetime of the astronauts. 
The “Cardiovascular Risk” encompasses specific in-mission and long-term health risks, 
with the overall severity and consequences largely dependent upon the duration of the partial 
gravity or weightlessness exposure and the level of radiation exposure (Figure 1). Mitigating 
factors include screening of astronauts before selection to the mission (pre-mission); the 
availability of countermeasures, medical monitoring capabilities, and medical intervention 
readiness (in-mission); and the state of medical technologies and medical practices (in-mission 
and long-term health). 

The current spaceflight evidence of in-mission risks includes the potential for the 
development of dysrhythmias (Section IX.A.1) and venous thromboembolism (Section IX.A.2). 
Despite previous reports describing the occurrence of dysrhythmias during spaceflight, the 
notion that exposure to weightlessness is a specific causative factor has been challenged 
(Convertino and Cooke 2005; Convertino 2009). Since the last evidence report on cardiac rhythm 
problems (Lee et al. 2017), Khine et al. (Khine et al. 2018) reported that, although there may be 
some individuals who are more susceptible, there is insufficient evidence to conclude that 6months in LEO causes the development of new, more frequent, or more complex dysrhythmias. 
These conclusions were based upon 48-hour Holter monitor recordings that encompassed the 
astronauts’ normal activities but did not capture cardiac rhythm during extravehicular activities 
(EVA) or preflight EVA training. Also, there has been an incidental finding of a thromboembolism 
in the IJV in one astronaut (Marshall-Goebel et al. 2019; Auñón-Chancellor et al. 2020). Continued 
surveillance of the astronauts (Pavela et al. 2022) will aid in determining whether this 
thromboembolism was an isolated incident or a risk for the broader population of astronauts. 
During re-entry and after landing on Earth after exposure to weightlessness, the primary 
cardiovascular risk is the development of hypotension leading to orthostatic intolerance (i.e., 
presyncope or syncope). This is particularly concerning if the astronauts are seated upright (e.g., 
during the Space Shuttle program), the astronauts are required to pilot the vehicle, or ground 
and medical support teams are not present at the landing site to help extract and assist the crew 
(e.g., off-nominal landing or landing on an extraterrestrial surface). The development of postspaceflight orthostatic intolerance is well documented, although the mechanisms are not yet 
fully understood (Section IX.A.3). Since the release of the last evidence report on orthostatic 
intolerance (Stenger et al. 2015), two research teams have documented that wearing lower body 
compression garments is an effective countermeasure to orthostatic intolerance in the first 24 
hours after landing from long-duration spaceflight (Fu et al. 2019; Lee et al. 2020c). However,

Astronauts undergo rigorous selection testing so that those who are chosen to the crews, 
particularly long-duration exploration crews, have a lower overall risk of cardiovascular events 
during a mission. iven t at astronauts represent an “above avera e” ealt y population, ave 
regular access to medical personnel and interventions, and that modifiable risk factors are closely 
monitored in the months leading up to launch, manifestation of CVD during a mission is unlikely. 
However, there is a concern that factors associated with spaceflight environment, such as 
exposure to psycho-social stress, impaired sleep, changes in musculoskeletal loading and in 
exercise routines, radiation exposure, and altered dietary practices, contribute to elevated levels 
of oxidative stress and inflammation (Section IX.A.4) and increase the lifetime risk of a CVD event 
or death. For example, although the prevalence of atrial flutter and fibrillation does not appear 
to be greater in astronauts than that seen in the general population, an expert panel convened 
by NASA (Barr 2010) suggested that astronauts present with these conditions at a younger age 
(Section IX.A.5). Atrial fibrillation is associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality and 
stroke, as well as a range of other cardiovascular events (Odutayo et al. 2016). Further, while it 
is generally accepted that astronauts are not at an increased risk of death due to CVD (Section 
IX.A.6), a recent retrospective analysis comparing astronauts to a well-matched healthy 
population (Charvat et al. 2022) suggested that they have a higher risk of having a cardiovascular 
event. The size of the astronaut population is comparatively small and NASA’s experience with 
long duration missions is relatively recent, thus continued surveillance of long-term 
cardiovascular outcomes is required to fully characterize the risk of developing CVD following 
spaceflight. 
Since the publication of the last evidence report describing the risk of CVD events (Lee et 
al. 2017), several studies have attempted to characterize the effects of spaceflight on the carotid 
artery as an index of atherosclerotic and stroke risk (Hughson et al. 2016; Arbeille et al. 2016, 
2017; Lee et al. 2020d), with some conflicting results. Whether measures of the carotid artery 
are the appropriate biomarker to ascertain cardiovascular risk in an astronaut population is 
unclear. Determining the appropriate biomarkers to ascertain CVD risk in individual astronauts is 
particularly difficult given that most risk calculators are based on results from older and less fit 
populations. Two recent studies have demonstrated the utility of combining traditional 
biomarkers with other cardiovascular health-related measures, such as high sensitivity C-reactive 
protein (hs-CRP) and coronary artery calcium (CAC) scoring, in predicting the 3- (Gore et al. 2020) 
and 10-year (Khera et al. 2018) risk of a CVD event in an apparently healthy population not 
exposed to spaceflight. 
The spaceflight environment is particularly challenging to conduct medical research. The 
operational demands of spaceflight missions limit the number of subjects and the crew time 
available to participate in studies and restrict the volume, mass, and power available to support 
science activities beyond that needed for vehicle functions and other mission-critical activities. 
Thus, evidence from ground-based investigations and spaceflight analogs (Section IX.B) 
contributes to the prediction of risks in each phase of the mission and of long-term health 
consequences (Section IX.A.7). Given that only the Apollo astronauts have ventured beyond LEO 
and that the durations of their exposures were short, most of the information about radiation 

exposure and the long-term health consequences is the result of ground-based research (Section 
IX.B.4), including epidemiological studies of atomic bomb survivors, radiation professionals, and 
radiation-therapy patients as well as animal models. While several studies since the previous 
report have used wild-type animals to study structural and functional impacts of space radiation 
on the cardiovascular system (Ramadan et al. 2016; Seawright et al. 2019; Sridharan et al. 2020; 
Nemec-Bakk et al. 2022), the majority of observed effects were sub-clinical and not functional 
outcomes that are not linked causally to CVD, which limits the translatability of the results. The 
challenges of performing standard human cardiac measurements/techniques in small animal 
models cannot be discounted. Assays (e.g., transthoracic echocardiography) which may be gold 
standard for detection of cardiac effects in human or larger animals, such as mini pigs, may not 
be appropriate for small animal use. A recent study by Bishawi et al. demonstrated the benefit 
of performing multi-modal measurements longitudinally to detect meaningful and functional 
cardiac decrements (Bishawi et al. 2022). The results collected from this study represent an 
important milestone in demonstrating how newer and more sensitive modalities and multimodal methodologies might improve translatability of animal research. 
IX. EVIDENCE 
A. Spaceflight Evidence 
1. Inflight Risk: Dysrhythmias 
The Mercury Program consisted of six single-person spaceflights, lasting as short as 15 
minutes to as long as 34 hours, with the first mission occurring in May 1961 and the final mission 
completed in May 1963 (Berry 1968). The Gemini Program encompassed 10 spaceflights from 
March 1965 to November 1966 (Berry 1968). Two astronauts participated in each flight in which 
the shortest mission was just under 5 hours and the longest mission was almost 4 days. Six of the 
missions included e trave icular activity ( V , “space al s” ; t e first V as performed on 
Gemini IV and lasted just 36 minutes. The longest EVA was performed on the final mission of the 
Gemini Program and was 5.5 hours long. There were no reports of significant dysrhythmias during 
these missions (Anzai et al. 2014). 
The pollo ro ram, N S ’s t ird uman spacefli t pro ram, consisted of three-man 
missions between 1968 (Apollo 7) and 1972 (Apollo 17) with the specific goal of landing humans 
on the Moon. Nine of the missions carried humans beyond LEO, and of these, six missions landed 
on the Moon. Each lunar landing mission, beginning with Apollo 11, also included EVAs, with each 
successive mission incorporating longer and more complex mission objectives. It was in the 
course of the Apollo program that the first clinically significant dysrhythmia was reported (Anzai 
et al. 2014). One crewmember during Apollo 15 experienced a 22-beat nodal bigeminal rhythm, 
which was followed by atrial premature beats (Rossum et al. 1997). This crewmember reported 
extreme fatigue during the incident, but only reported the incident when questioned by crew 
surgeons. Twenty-one months later the crewmember suffered from coronary artery disease and 
a cardiac infarction (Leguay and Seigneuric 1981). 

S ylab as N S ’s first space station; it as visited by three separate three-man crews 
between May 1973 and February 1974 with mission lengths of 28, 59, and 84 days. During the 
Skylab missions, several instances of premature ventricular complexes (PVCs), supraventricular 
PVCs, and nodal dysrhythmia were recorded. The dysrhythmias occurred during effort tests, 
EVAs, lower body negative pressure (LBNP) sessions, and were not specific to any particular phase 
of the mission or activity. These included two consecutive PVCs when an astronaut was exercising 
and an episode of atrioventricular dissociation preceded by sinus bradycardia in two astronauts 
(Leguay and Seigneuric 1981). 
One hundred and thirty-five Space Shuttle missions occurred over the course of 30 years 
between April 12, 1981, and July 8, 2011, beginning with Space Transportation System (STS) 1 
and ending with STS-135. During the first four flights (called test flights), ECG was monitored 
continuously, and frequent PVCs were observed in crewmembers during re-entry and landing 
(Charles et al. 1996). In subsequent missions, routine ECG monitoring was conducted only during 
EVA. According to Charles et al. (Charles et al. 1996), one third of the astronauts who flew before 
1996 experienced either premature atrial complexes (PACs) or PVCs while participating in EVA. 
Notable ECG findings during EVA were reported in two crewmembers: one with 10 minutes of 
sustained ventricular bigeminy and the other with episodes of frequent PACs. 
Two studies of cardiac rhythm disturbances were performed in response to medical 
reports of dysrhythmias occurring in 9 of 14 Shuttle EVA astronauts between 1983 and 1985. 
Rossum et al. (Rossum et al. 1997) reviewed 24-hour Holter recordings acquired during and after 
high altitude chamber training, underwater training for EVA (Weightless Environment Training 
Facility; WETF), 30 days before launch, during and after each EVA, and on return to Earth. The 
investigators observed no change in the number of PVCs or PACs per hour during flight compared 
to preflight or postflight. Fritsch-Yelle et al. (Fritsch-Yelle et al. 1996a) collected 24-hour Holter 
recordings from 12 astronauts before, during, and after six Space Shuttle missions lasting 5-10 
days. The number of PVCs per hour decreased from pre- to inflight, and there was a trend 
towards a decrease in the number of PACs per hour. Some astronauts experienced no 
dysrhythmias throughout the study. 
In the NASA-Mir program, in which seven NASA astronauts participated in seven longduration missions on the Mir Space Station with 12 Russian cosmonauts, ECG monitoring 
revealed episodic dysrhythmias in 10 astronauts and cosmonauts, with a wide range of incidence 
across crewmembers (Gontcharov et al. 2005). Atrial and ventricular dysrhythmias were 
observed when crewmembers were at rest, exercising, and performing EVA. All 10 astronauts 
and cosmonauts who experienced atrial or ventricular dysrhythmias during their mission had 
similar findings during their preflight training, although the episodes were perhaps less frequent 
or severe before launch. In-flight Holter monitoring recorded an isolated incident of a nonsustained 14-beat ventricular tachycardia in one of the crew member, with a maximum heart 
rate of 215 beats per minute (Fritsch-Yelle et al. 1998). This crewmember experienced a single 
occurrence of a similar event during a previous mission (Gontcharov et al. 2005). Although not 
part of a systematic scientific study, this case raised concerns of dysrhythmias during longduration spaceflight (Baevsky et al. 2007b). 

The ISS has been continuously inhabited since the arrival of Expedition 1 on November 2, 
2000. In a prospective study Khine et al. collected 48-hour Holter recordings in 13 astronauts (9 
men, 4 women) before, during, and after 6-month missions (Khine et al. 2018). There was no 
statistically significant increase in the number of premature atrial contractions (PACs) from 
preflight, although one astronaut had an increase in supraventricular ectopic beats, and another 
astronaut experienced an increase in ventricular dysrhythmias. Further, none of the astronauts 
developed atrial fibrillation, even though left atrial size increased, a risk factor for the 
development of atrial fibrillation, when measured with MRI within 5 days after landing. However, 
right atrial size was not different than preflight. The investigators have suggested that there could 
be a subset of individuals who are more susceptible to an increased incidence of dysrhythmias. 
A key element for future exploration missions may be to identify the phenotype and the stressors 
that could be responsible for the increases in dysrhythmias that occur in certain individuals 
(Romero et al. 2015). Further, there may latent, not yet understood, effects of spaceflight 
exposure that contribute to a higher risk of a cardiovascular event (Charvat et al. 2022). 
Khine et al. (Khine et al. 2018) did not measure atrial dimensions during spaceflight but 
others have shown that the cephalad fluid shift upon entry into weightlessness (Thornton et al. 
1977, 1987) initially results in distension of the cardiac chambers (Buckey et al. 1996a; Videbaek 
and Norsk 1997; Corsi et al. 2002; Caiani et al. 2006). This resolves somewhat (Arbeille et al. 2001) 
as the body fluids are redistributed and blood and plasma volume decrease (Leach et al. 1996; 
Meck et al. 2001). Reflecting this, stroke volume and cardiac output initially increase in 
weightlessness but then decrease over the first few days of flight (Prisk et al. 1993). Thus, stroke 
volume and cardiac output during spaceflight is similar to preflight resting supine levels (Herault 
et al. 2000; Hamilton et al. 2011) but greater than seated rest (Shykoff et al. 1996; Norsk et al. 
2015). Further, although not a direct measure of atrial distension, IJV cross-sectional area also is 
increased during short- and long-duration spaceflight (Herault et al. 2000; Arbeille et al. 2001), 
and internal jugular venous pressure is increased during parabolic flight (Martin et al. 2016) and 
during long-duration spaceflight compared to the seated posture (Marshall-Goebel et al. 2019). 
Additionally, observations from parabolic flight and spaceflight (Summers et al. 2010; Khine et al. 
2018) suggest that the cardiac chambers become more spherical, potentially contributing to 
cardiac remodeling and changes in cardiac electrophysiology. 
A large amount of Soviet-era spaceflight results, including human research data, have not 
been published nor have the observations widely disseminated (Bogomolov et al. 2009). Until 
the Apollo-Soyuz Test Project, space medicine practices at NASA and in the Soviet (later Russian) 
space programs developed independently. Cooperation and exchange of information between 
the two space programs manifested as the US-USSR Joint Working Group on Biology and 
 edicine in t e early 970’s, and the NASA and Russian space programs jointly prepared for the 
NASA- ir ro ram in t e early 990’s. 992 a reement bet een t e United States (US) and 
the Russian Federation paved the way for a series of joint spaceflight missions from 1994 to 1998 
(Bogomolov et al. 2009). Joint missions included seven Russian cosmonauts participating in Space 
Shuttle missions and seven NASA astronauts participating in long-duration missions on the Mir 
Space Station. 

Romanov et al. (Romanov et al. 1987) reviewed electrophysiology results from Soviet and 
Russian spaceflight missions between 1964 and 1985 and reported observations of 
supraventricular and ventricular premature beats, with the highest reported frequency occurring 
during launch and the first hours of the mission. Whereas most in-flight dysrhythmias were not 
considered to be clinically significant, one cosmonaut was returned before the planned end of 
his mission due to persistent supraventricular tachycardia (Bogomolov et al. 2009). ECG changes 
that were observed after these missions generally resolved after flight, but three cosmonauts 
later demonstrated ECG changes consistent with ischemic heart disease (Romanov et al. 1987). 
In a separate study, two cosmonauts who completed a one-year mission on the Mir Space Station 
participated in a 24-hour Holter data collection in the first day after landing. Each cosmonaut had 
infrequent episodes of isolated PVCs (Grigoriev et al. 1991). A review of ECG records from 30 
cosmonauts who participated in long-duration missions (73 to 197 days) on either Mir or ISS 
determined that the majority of the crewmembers (70%) did not exhibit dysrhythmias during 
preflight training, launch, or re-entry, and a few (10%) who had dysrhythmias preflight did not 
have dysrhythmias during launch or landing (Kotovskaia et al. 2008). However, a small number 
of individuals had increased frequencies of rhythm and conduction disturbances during re-entry 
that were not observed during preflight training. 
Launching into and landing from space are significant physical and psychological stressors 
that historically were significant sources of concern. Kotovskaia et al. (Kotovskaia and Vil’-Villiams 
2007) reported that individuals who exhibited dysrhythmias in the preflight period also had 
similar dysrhythmias during launch and entry into weightlessness. Comparing data from nonprofessional cosmonaut visitors (29 to 60 years old) and medically-qualified cosmonauts (31-49 
years old) obtained during re-entry on the Soyuz after short stays on ISS (8 to 12 days), Kotovskaia 
et al. (Kotovs aia and Vil’-Villiams 2007) found the non-professional flyers, particularly the older 
ISS visitors, had higher heart rates and more frequent dysrhythmias than the cosmonauts. Less 
rigorous medical screening for non-professional space visitors may have contributed to this 
disparity. 
There is not sufficient evidence to conclude that exposure to weightlessness alone 
precipitates cardiac dysrhythmias, although some factors that have been associated with 
dysrhythmias are modified by spaceflight. Some of the previously identified dysrhythmias 
resulted from pre-existing, undiagnosed disease. Additional pre-selection tests to screen 
astronauts and cosmonauts before selection and flight assignments, including coronary artery 
calcium scoring, have been added to reduce the likelihood such occurrences (Hamilton et al. 
2005, 2006). 
a. Q-T Intervals 
The Q-T interval is a measure of the combined duration of ventricular depolarization 
(QRS) and repolarization (T-wave). The QRS complex is usually of fixed duration in healthy 
individuals. Thus, changes in QT duration represent alterations in ventricular repolarization. The 
Q-T interval of the surface ECG is a spatial and temporal summation of all cardiac cellular action 
potentials. Not all cells within the heart share identical action potentials; therefore, a certain 

degree of variability, or inhomogeneity exists in their repolarization time. The degree of 
inhomogeneity during repolarization directly correlates with the overall morphology of the Q-T 
waveform (primarily the T-wave) and in most cases with the Q-T interval duration. A clear 
association between the magnitude of inhomogeneity of repolarization and the risk for the 
development of ventricular dysrhythmias has been established (Lux et al. 2001; El-Sherif 2001; 
Shusterman et al. 2006). 
The Q-T interval is often corrected for heart rate and is shown as Q-Tc. Some conditions 
that can prolong the Q-Tc interval are ischemic heart disease, autonomic dysfunction, 
bradycardia, electrolyte abnormalities, cardiac remodeling, and medications that interfere with 
the cardiac potassium ion channels (Ishida et al. 1995; Lo et al. 1996; Savelieva et al. 1999; 
Haverkamp et al. 2000; Khan 2002). It is known that astronauts develop changes in the autonomic 
nervous system (Fritsch et al. 1992; Fritsch-Yelle et al. 1996b; Meck et al. 2001; Rossum et al. 
2001), and that on long-duration flights astronauts have a relative bradycardia compared to 
astronauts on short-duration flights (Meck et al. 2001). Further, there is evidence of cardiac 
remodeling after long-duration flights (Perhonen et al. 2001a). Finally, there are medications 
available to astronauts aboard the ISS that prolong Q-Tc interval, including ciprofloxacin, haldol, 
inderal, verapamil, zithromax, Zoloft®, and nortriptyline. This combination of factors might cause 
or exacerbate the prolongation of the Q-T interval. 
Prolongation of Q-Tc interval does not itself guarantee an increase in ventricular 
dysrhythmias. For example, sleep, hypothyroidism, and use of the anti-arrhythmic drug 
amiodarone all prolong Q-Tc without increasing the incidence of ventricular dysrhythmias. 
Prolongation of the Q-Tc interval in astronauts has been documented on several occasions but it 
is not clear if these findings have any clinical significance or portend risk ( ’ unno et al. 2003; 
Mitchell and Meck, 2004). 
b. Electrolyte Disturbances 
Cardiac rhythm abnormalities and exercise intolerance observed in the Apollo 15 Lunar 
Module Pilot were attributed to electrolyte disturbances, particularly hypokalemia (Leguay and 
Seigneuric 1981). Inadequate potassium intake was suspected; thus, before Apollo 16 a 12-day 
bed rest study with low potassium intake was conducted with two subjects as a simulation of the 
Apollo 15 mission (Hyatt et al. 1975). Neither of the bed rest subjects developed symptomatic 
hypokalemia, only one demonstrated minor ECG abnormalities, and the decrease in exercise 
capacity and orthostatic tolerance was no more than expected for a bed rest of similar duration 
with normal dietary intakes. Serum and urinary potassium levels decreased by 7% and 47%, 
respectively, in both Apollo crewmembers (Leach et al. 1975) 
A later review of the Apollo 15 mission suggested that magnesium deficiency contributed 
to the dysrhythmias and other symptoms (Rowe 1998). However, while not specific to Apollo 15, 
Smith and Zwart (Smith and Zwart, 2015) report that during long-duration ISS missions, urinary 
magnesium concentrations are elevated within the first 2 weeks of the mission and serum levels 
are elevated above preflight levels after 3-4 months of spaceflight. Both urinary and serum 

magnesium decrease and are lower than preflight on landing day then return to preflight levels 
within 1 month after landing. Despite these dynamics, tissue levels of magnesium, measured in 
epithelial cells from sublingual scrapes, are not different from preflight on landing day or 1 month 
after landing. After short-duration spaceflight, neither urinary nor serum magnesium levels are 
different than preflight. 
Prolonged Q-Tc intervals observed after long-duration spaceflight were not associated 
with clinically-relevant changes in electrolyte levels ( ’ unno et al. 200 . In five Shuttle and 
seven Mir astronauts, there was no pre- to postflight change in serum magnesium or calcium 
levels; although serum potassium measured on landing day was significantly less than preflight, 
the levels were still within clinical normative ranges. 
c. Catecholamines 
Elevated catecholamines and increased sympathetic activity have been associated with 
the development of dysrhythmias in terrestrial populations (Leguay and Seigneuric 1981). Leach 
et al. (Leach et al. 1996) reported no change in serum catecholamines from pre- to in-flight and 
that urinary norepinephrine levels were lower during Space Shuttle missions than before launch. 
Norsk et al. (Norsk et al. 2015) reported no change in norepinephrine and epinephrine levels from 
preflight (seated) to in-flight during 3-6 month ISS missions.

and result in local hypoxic endothelial activation. Additional common risk factors for the 
development of VTE on Earth include increasing age, active cancer, trauma, infection,

due to the increased risk of developing venous thromboembolism. Patients on Earth with

compared to those with albumin concentrations >4.2 g/dL (Chi et al. 2019). When combined with

cOC users had one or more serum albumin measures <3.8 g/dL, the low end of the normal clinical

hypoalbuminemia, cOC users demonstrated increased inflammation markers and elevated levels 
of transferrin, which terrestrially correlate to an increased risk of DVT or VTE. These data highlight 
the need to consider the type of cOC used during spaceflight since third-generation cOC impart 
a greater risk of developing VTE than second-generation cOC (Conard 1999; Kemmeren et al. 
2001). 
Further potential risks of VTE in the spaceflight environment include hematologic index 
alterations (reduced plasma volume and increased hematocrit and platelet concentration), 

inflammation, thrombophilia, and changes in vascular structure/function (Kunz et al. 2017; 
Crucian et al. 2018). Moreover, abnormal flow characteristics in microgravity (reduced peak 
velocities, retrograde flow, and increased blood echogenicity), some of which may be due to 
compression of the jugular vein by the carotid artery in microgravity, may contribute to increased 
risk for thrombosis formation in some individuals, especially in the left IJV (Pavela et al. 2022).

biomarkers for risk of VTE, such as d-dimer, fragmin f1.2, coagulation cascade, V-CAM-1 and Eselectin, because some terrestrial predictors for VTE, including high body mass index and medical 
comorbidities, generally do not apply to astronauts due to fitness and healthy lifestyle (Jain et al. 
2020).

3. Post-flight Risk: Orthostatic Intolerance 
Post-spaceflight orthostatic intolerance was evident, even after short stays in 
weightlessness, during the Mercury (1961 to 1963) and Gemini (1965 to 1966) missions. The 
Mercury-Atlas 9 pilot became hypotensive during a 70° head-up tilt test after only 34 hours of 
flight. Similarly, orthostatic intolerance has been observed after 24 hours of bed rest (Nixon et 
al. 1979; Blomqvist et al. 1980), a spaceflight analog (Fortney et al. 2010). Elevated heart rate, 
decreased pulse pressure, and increased fluid pooling in the lower extremities were consistently 
observed during tilt testing in Mercury and Gemini astronauts for up to 50 hours after 
splashdown following spaceflight missions of 3 to 14 days (1). 
The larger spacecraft volume and increased mobility in the Apollo missions were 
predicted to reduce post-spaceflight orthostatic intolerance compared to the Mercury program 
but orthostatic intolerance still was prevalent (Hoffler et al. 1974; Hoffler and Johnson, 1975). Of 
the 21 Apollo astronauts that performed pre- and post-spaceflight orthostatic tolerance tests, 13 
exhibited an increased heart rate when at rest postflight. Resting heart rate returned to preflight 
values by the third examination, two to three days after splashdown. During immediate postflight 
orthostatic evaluations, astronauts exhibited increased heart rate and decreased stroke volume, 
systolic and pulse pressures which were greater than those responses before flight. Additionally, 
body weight, calf circumference, and cardiothoracic ratio were all decreased immediately 
postflight. These measurements had not returned to their preflight values by the third postflight 

examination, suggesting the changes were not entirely due to fluid loss (Hoffler and Johnson 
1975). The findings of the Apollo Program aided the understanding of cardiovascular changes 
during spaceflight in preparation for longer duration spaceflight in the Skylab missions. 
Each of the Skylab missions (1973 to 1974) set new records for amount of time spent in 
space (28, 59, and 84 days) by NASA astronauts. The larger spacecraft and longer duration of the 
missions allowed the Skylab program to assess the effects of spaceflight on more physiological 
parameters. During Skylab missions LBNP was used as an orthostatic tolerance test pre- and postspaceflight, as well as during the mission. Inflight LBNP revealed the existence of orthostatic 
intolerance after 4 to 6 days of flight (Johnson et al. 1977b). Astronauts experienced a greater 
stress during inflight LBNP than preflight LBNP (Johnson and Bergman 1977), illustrated by their 
greater increases in heart rate and leg volume and greater decreases in systolic blood pressure 
(Johnson et al. 1977b). Inflight LBNP also served as a predictor of the degree of postflight 
orthostatic intolerance, which aided crew health care after long-duration missions. 
Results from Gemini and Apollo had suggested a decrease in cardiac function due to 
spaceflight, raising concerns about potential detrimental effects of long duration spaceflight on 
the cardiovascular system. Postflight clinical data suggested there might be an impediment to 
venous return as well as a myocardial effect causing decreased cardiovascular function (Johnson 
and Bergman 1977). Two of the three astronauts in Skylab 4 had decreased stroke volume and 
cardiac output upon their return to Earth, yet the recovery of cardiac volume and mass in the 
first month after landing to preflight values led to the conclusion that 84 days in space is not a 
long-enough time to produce irreversible cardiac dysfunction (Henry et al. 1977). 

The Extended Duration Orbiter Medical Project (EDOMP; 1989 to 1995) aimed to define 
the effects of short-duration spaceflight in a more controlled environment aboard the Space 
Shuttle Orbiter with a larger subject pool and develop appropriate countermeasures (Charles et 
al. 1999). Several studies included 24-hour Holter monitoring, blood pressure recordings, and 
two-dimensional echocardiography measured inflight, and heart rate and blood pressures were 
measured during launch and reentry. Inflight heart rate and systolic and diastolic blood pressure 
were decreased when compared to the preflight values (Figure 5). Upon re-entry, these values 
increased past their preflight baseline, reaching maximal values at peak gravity (Charles et al. 
1999). During standing after touchdown, both systolic and diastolic pressures significantly 
decreased from the seated value, and the decrease in diastolic pressure was greater in the 
crewmembers who did not inflate their G-suits. Systolic pressure and heart rate returned to 
preflight values within an hour of landing, whereas all other spaceflight-induced cardiovascular 
changes were reversed within a week after landing.

A review of tilt tests results from US astronauts after ISS and Space Shuttle missions also

associated with orthostatic hypotension, specifically stroke volume and diastolic blood pressure,

following short-duration Space Shuttle missions. Astronauts are monitored closely during 
postflight activities, and flight surgeons guide astronauts to avoid activities that may provoke the 
onset of orthostatic hypotension. While orthostatic intolerance becomes less prevalent 24 hours 
after landing, partially through the restoration of plasma volume (Mulavara et al. 2018), some 
astronauts continue to experience symptoms for several days (Lee et al. 2015). It is not 
uncommon for some astronauts and cosmonauts to wear lower body compression garments in 
the first week after landing to manage symptoms, including lower leg swelling (Stenger et al. 
2014). 
The vast majority of astronauts have been male (Smith et al. 2020), and consequently any 
conclusions drawn regarding the physiological responses to spaceflight are male-biased. Some 
significant differences in how men and women respond to spaceflight exist (Harm et al. 2001; 
Platts et al. 2014), including sex differences in the effects of spaceflight on cardiovascular 
responses to orthostatic stress (Waters et al. 2002). Greater than 80% of female crewmembers 
became presyncopal during a postflight tilt test (Waters et al. 2002) compared to about 20% of 
men, even when pilots, whose occupational success includes the ability to tolerate high g-forces, 
were removed from the analyses. 
In a recent study, Fu et al. (2019) observed no hypotensive events in 12 astronauts (four 
women) during the first 24 hours after landing following prolonged spaceflight with use of various 
countermeasures (suit cooling, fluid loading, compression garments, and inflight exercise) (Fu et 
al. 2019). However, interpretation of these results is limited because the analyses did not 
consider whether the astronaut was supine or upright or whether they were wearing 
compression garments. Further, most astronauts returning from the ISS receive intravenous 
fluids within hours of landing (Mulavara et al. 2018). A recent study of ISS astronauts showed 
that compression garments worn on landing day have a significant benefit to reducing the 
cardiovascular stress associated with the upright posture (Mulavara et al. 2018; Lee et al. 2020c), 
including the immediate post-landing period before intravenous fluids were administered. 

a. Plasma and Blood Volume 
The etiology of orthostatic intolerance is complicated and multifactorial. Decreased 
intravascular volume, secondary to the headward fluid shift that occurs in space (Thornton et al. 
1977, 1987), is likely an initiating event. One of the first physiological changes noted during the 
Apollo program was the decrease in plasma volume, exhibited by the decrease in weight of the 
crewmen (Leach et al. 1975). One third of the average 5% weight loss was regained within 24 
hours postflight, suggesting this fractional change was due to a loss of fluid. The remainder of the 
body weight loss was attributed to tissue loss, which is characterized by a longer recovery time 
(Leach et al. 1975). Apollo results suggested that the degree of plasma volume loss was 
independent of flight duration, later supported by a finding of no difference in plasma volume 
loss after two weeks and after six months of spaceflight. In contrast, average plasma volume 
losses were reported to be higher in the Skylab 2, 3, and 4 missions than in the Apollo program, 
with average losses of 8.4%, 13.1% and 15.9%, respectively (Johnson et al. 1977a). However, 
postflight orthostatic hypotension and presyncope are not dependent on the degree of postflight 
hypovolemia alone. For example, there was no difference in plasma volume loss between men 
who were presyncopal and non-presyncopal during tilt testing on landing day after shortduration missions (Waters et al. 2002). Further, despite no differences in plasma volume loss 
between short- and long-duration spaceflight (Figure 8), the incidence of orthostatic intolerance 
after long-duration spaceflight is much greater (Meck et al. 2001). 
The mechanism of the plasma volume loss has been a matter of some debate (Norsk, 2005). 
There have been limited inflight studies of plasma volume. One study demonstrated a decrease 
in total body water during flight, suggesting but not proving a diuresis (Leach et al. 1991). A 
second study reported a decrease in plasma volume, but an increase in intracellular fluid, 
su estin “ rd spacin ” and not a diuresis (Leach et al. 1996). However, postflight studies from 
the Apollo (Leach et al. 1975) and Space Shuttle missions (Greenisen et al. 1999) programs do 
not show an increase in the intracellular fluid compartment. These disparate flight data reinforce 
the need for further flight studies.

Red blood cell count, hemoglobin concentration, and hematocrit are elevated during 
short- and long-duration spaceflight (Alfrey et al. 1996; Kunz et al. 2017), perhaps due to a 
disproportionate decrease in plasma volume (Leach et al. 1996). Hemolysis, particularly of young 
red blood cells (Alfrey et al. 1996), contributes to a decrease in red cell mass that persists 
throughout spaceflight such that astronauts are considered to be clinically anemic after landing 
(Trudel et al. 2022). However, these data must be considered within the dynamic of the 
restoration of plasma volume, which is more rapid (Leach et al. 1996) and actively manipulated 
by end-of-mission fluid loading and intravenous fluids after landing. Erythropoietin is decreased 
during short-duration spaceflight (Udden et al. 1995) though it appears to return to preflight 
levels in longer duration missions (Trudel et al. 2022). Within the first few days after landing, 
hemolysis is decreased (Trudel et al. 2022) and erythropoietin is increased (Alfrey et al. 1996) but 
restoration of red cell mass is not apparent until at least 30 days postflight (Johnson et al. 1977a). 
Red blood cell production and hemolysis abated but still were higher than preflight one year after 
landing (Trudel et al. 2022). 
b. Other Factors 
The downstream effects and the physiological responses (or lack thereof) are likely the 
primary drivers of orthostatic intolerance after weightlessness. While all crewmembers who have 
been tested are hypovolemic on landing day, only a fraction of them develop orthostatic 
intolerance during testing. In men who lost an equal amount of plasma volume, astronauts who 
had a hyper-adrenergic response to orthostasis did not become presyncopal (Waters et al. 2002). 
Dysfunction of the sympathetic nervous system (Goldstein et al. 2003), with or without failure of 
the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (Jacob et al. 1997) could contribute to post-spaceflight 
orthostatic intolerance. 
It is widely accepted that the inability to elevate peripheral vascular resistance following 
spaceflight contributes to the orthostatic intolerance experienced by many astronauts. Lower 
body peripheral resistance vessels have a larger cross-sectional area (increased capacitance) 
(Tuday et al. 2007) and a diminished ability to constrict following exposure to simulated 
microgravity (Delp et al. 2000). Furthermore, in arteries from non-posture related muscles, the

lower constrictor function is related to a blunted response to vasoconstrictor stimuli (Delp et al. 
1993; Behnke et al. 2008) and reduced myogenic tone (Purdy et al. 1998). Postflight data 
measuring muscle sympathetic nerve activity in six non-presyncopal male astronauts (Levine et 
al. 2002) shows that sympathetic responses in these crewmembers are appropriate. 
Unfortunately, there were no presyncopal subjects in this study and the postflight sympathetic 
dysfunction in that group of astronauts could not be duplicated. 
Furthermore, astronauts who experienced both short- and then long-duration spaceflight 
were more likely to have a hypo-adrenergic response and become presyncopal during tilt testing 
after the long-duration flight despite similar plasma volume losses in both flights (Meck et al. 
2001). Thus, it is not the plasma volume loss alone that causes presyncope, but the lack of an 
adequate physiological response to the hypovolemia. There also appears to be a correlation 
between the vestibular system, which is altered after long-duration spaceflight and the 
sympathetic reflex to orthostatic stress upon return from the ISS. This vestibulo-sympathetic 
response has been demonstrated to be damped after long-duration spaceflight (Deshpande et 
al. 2020). 
Cardiac atrophy and the resulting decrease in stroke volume (Levine et al. 1997; Perhonen 
et al. 2001a) also may play a role in postflight orthostatic intolerance. Stroke volume is altered 
by mechanical and hydrostatic effects (Negishi et al. 2017) and serves as the primary stimulus to 
the baroreflex regulation of arterial pressure during an orthostatic stress (Levine et al. 1991). 
Orthostatic hypotension will ensue if the fall in stroke volume is of sufficient magnitude to 
overwhelm normal compensatory mechanisms or if the reflex increase in heart rate and total 
peripheral resistance is impaired by adaptation of the autonomic nervous system (Robertson et 
al. 1994). After adaptation to real or simulated microgravity, virtually all individuals studied have 
an excessive fall in stroke volume in the upright position (Buckey et al. 1996b; Fritsch-Yelle et al. 
1996b), although there are conflicting data regarding changes in baroreflex regulation of heart 
rate and vascular resistance (Convertino et al. 1990; Eckberg and Fritsch 1992; Fritsch et al. 1992; 
Convertino et al. 1994; Hughson et al. 1994a; Fritsch-Yelle et al. 1994; Crandall et al. 1994; Iwasaki 
et al. 2000, 2004). 
Heart rate variability (HRV) and blood pressure variability provide a means to 
noninvasively approximate autonomic activity through analysis of fluctuations in the R-R interval 
and systolic blood pressure (SBP) data. In particular, the high frequency component of the R-R 
interval series (HF-RR), in absolute or normalized units, is an index of parasympathetic 
modulation (Pomeranz et al. 1985; Pagani et al. 1986; Task Force of the European Society of 
Cardiology the North American Society of Pacing 1996), and the low frequency component of the 
SBP (LF-SBP) approximates sympathetic activity (Preiss and Polosa 1974; Pagani et al. 1986; 
Malliani et al. 1991; Tanaka et al. 2013). The interpretation of the low frequency to high 
frequency ratio of the R-R interval (LF/HF-RR) is more controversial. The LF/HF-RR is often used 
as a marker of sympathovagal balance (Pagani et al. 1986; Malliani et al. 1991; Montano et al. 
1994; Task Force of the European Society of Cardiology the North American Society of Pacing 
1996). However, this interpretation has been challenged by studies that question the concept 
and construction of the index (Eckberg 1997), suggest that variation in the LF/HF-RR is partially 

due to changes in baroreflex function (Goldstein et al. 2011), or conclude that spectral HRV is 
mainly a measure of parasympathetic activity (Reyes Del Paso et al. 2013). 
While a great deal of research has been focused on understanding and overcoming the 
cardiovascular deconditioning effects of the vasculature in the lower body, recent evidence 
suggests that changes in cerebral vascular conductance also may contribute to the incidence of 
orthostatic intolerance in astronauts. In general, cerebral blood flow is well protected against 
systemic changes in pressure and flow without compromising the high demands of brain tissue. 
Unfortunately, few spaceflight studies have been designed to examine the role of cerebral blood 
flow and cerebral autoregulation in humans. Much of the data which does exist argues against 
changes cerebral autoregulation and do not provide evidence for changes in vascular function. 
For example, Fritsch-Yelle et al. (Fritsch-Yelle et al. 1996b) measured blood flow velocity in the 
middle cerebral artery using ultrasound but did not find a difference in tilt response between 
presyncopal and nonpresyncopal astronauts. In a similar study, measures of cerebral blood flow, 
velocities, and beat-to-beat changes in arterial pressure were collected before and after 16 days 
in space. The authors of this study maintained that static autoregulation was not impaired and 
dynamic regulation was actually improved (Iwasaki et al. 2007). Greaves et al. reported similar 
findings in subjects following 60 days of head-down tilt bed rest (Greaves et al. 2007). 
c. Current Countermeasures: Inflight Exercise 
Inflight exercise was initiated by NASA as a test of exercise tolerance during the Mercury 
program and was recommended for future missions to maintain orthostatic responses and egress 
capability in the event of an emergency soon after landing (Moore et al. 2010). The exercise 
hardware that is available today began its development in the Space Shuttle program and more 
advanced systems have been employed on ISS (Hayes et al. 2013; Korth 2015). Inflight exercise 
time was allotted for the Space Shuttle crews but specific exercise was not prescribed by the 
NASA specialists until the NASA-Mir and ISS missions (Lee et al. 2019). Currently, ISS crews are 
scheduled 2.5 hours per day of time in which to prepare for exercise (don exercise clothes and 
set-up equipment , perform continuous or interval style “aerobic” e ercise and resistive e ercise, 
and clean-up (personal hygiene, stow exercise hardware). Exercise countermeasures are 
primarily intended to maintain aerobic and muscular fitness, musculoskeletal and cardiovascular 
health, and performance (Smith et al. 2012; Moore et al. 2014; Lee et al. 2019). 
Few spaceflight studies have specifically addressed whether inflight exercise affects the 
post-spaceflight orthostatic responses. In 30 Space Shuttle astronauts who self-selected their 
inflight exercise habits, those who performed moderate to vigorous aerobic exercise regularly 
during their mission attenuated the postflight tachycardia normally seen with standing on landing 
day (Lee et al. 1999). However, increasing the volume or intensity of inflight exercise did not 
decrease the number of astronauts who became presyncopal while standing on landing day. In a 
separate investigation, cardiac output and stroke volume was better maintained during a 10minute stand test in four Space Shuttle astronauts who performed a bout of maximal exercise 24 
hours before landing than in four subjects who performed a minimal amount of exercise (Moore 

et al. 2001). Maximal exercise was previously demonstrated to restore plasma volume 
(Convertino et al. 1996)and protect LBNP tolerance (Engelke et al. 1996) after 16 days of bed rest. 
Exercise countermeasures, in general, are expected to protect cardiovascular function 
and postflight egress capability. In a recent study of ISS astronauts, the decrease in peak oxygen 
consumption was less (-6%) in astronauts who perform vigorous exercise (English et al. 2020) 
compared to that previously reported (-15%) (Moore et al. 2014). A previous long-duration bed 
rest study demonstrated that cardiac mass and function are protected by a vigorous exercise 
countermeasure protocol similar to that used by astronauts (Scott et al. 2018). 
d. Current Countermeasures: Fluid Loading 
All astronauts returning from space on a NASA vehicle are required to ingest a “fluid load” 
of an isotonic solution consisting of salt tablets and water or an electrolyte solution. The efficacy 
of this countermeasure following Shuttle flights was evaluated by Bungo et al. (Bungo et al. 1985). 
Heart rate and blood pressure were measured during a passive stand test before and after 
spaceflight (54 to 194 hours postflight). Both variables, as well as a cardiovascular index of 
deconditioning (CID), were significantly improved, but not fully protected to preflight levels, 
when astronauts used the fluid loading countermeasure compared to control subjects who did 
not use fluid loading (Figure 10). However, the efficacy of the fluid loading countermeasure to 
mitigate plasma and blood volume losses was not measured. 
In 1985, consuming fluid and salt prior to landing (fluid loading) became a medical 
requirement, although the level of adherence to the countermeasure has not been clearly 
documented. Astronauts continued to return from space with plasma volume deficits ranging 
from 5 to 19% after Space Shuttle missions (Leach et al. 1996; Buckey et al. 1996b; Fritsch-Yelle

e. Current Countermeasures: Compression Garments 
The Apollo 16 and 17 missions introduced countermeasures for orthostatic intolerance in 
the form of anti-hypotensive garments (Hoffler and Johnson 1975). Though the countermeasure 
appeared to provide moderate protection against orthostatic hypotension, testing in Apollo 16 
was plagued with problems. The countermeasure for this flight was Jobst compression stockings 
with a pressure of 40-45 mmHg at the ankle and linearly decreasing pressure to 10 mmHg at the 
waist. The confined space inside the spacecraft prevented the crewmembers from donning the 
stockings inflight, and the stockings were only worn for a stand test after the postflight LBNP 
orthostatic tolerance test. Additionally, the stockings could not be fitted accurately for postflight 
testing due to the unquantifiable decrease in leg circumference. Finally, postflight testing was 
performed with ambient temperatures 10°C higher than preflight testing, augmenting the 
orthostatic stresses. Conversely, different countermeasure and testing conditions in Apollo 17 
successfully prevented heart rate changes during LBNP. In this mission, the orthostatic test was 
performed in the air-conditioned Skylab Mobile Laboratory, and the anti-hypotension suit was 
an inflatable suit which applied lower body positive pressure from the ankles to the waist but 
was only used by a single crewmember. This crewmember donned the garment before reentry, 

inflated it after splashdown while still in the spacecraft, and did not deflate the suit until ten 
minutes had elapsed in the passive stand test. Though the crewmember did not exhibit the 
typical change in heart rate during LBNP, it should be noted that he also did not follow the trend 
in cardiothoracic ratio change and postflight limb volume changes (Hoffler and Johnson 1975). 
Astronauts and cosmonauts wear lower compression garments during re-entry to gravity 
(Figure 11). These garments compress the capacitance vessels, thereby promoting venous return. 
Lower body compression garments were not routinely worn by astronauts on NASA space 
vehicles until the Space Shuttle program. In astronauts (n=24) who inflated their anti-G suit (AGS) 
during re-entry landing, arterial pressure was less than those who did not inflate the AGS (n=8), 
although heart rate was not different (Perez et al. 2003) (Figure 12).

The Cardiovascular and Vision Laboratory at NASA Johnson Space Center has been testing 
different configurations of lower body compression garments (e.g., area of coverage, amount of 
compression) for use during exploration missions. Thigh-high garments (55 mmHg of 
compression at the ankle, decreasing linearly to 18 mmHg at the knee and to 6 mmHg at the top 
of the leg) were custom fitted to five Space Shuttle crew members (Stenger et al. 2010). Thighhigh garments provided some protection to orthostatic intolerance after a short duration Space 
Shuttle mission, but the lack of abdominal compression may have limited their efficacy (Denq et 
al. 1997). Despite evidence that leg-only compression can ameliorate vasovagal syncope (Dos 
Santos et al. 2013), it is generally accepted that leg-only compression is insufficient (Protheroe 
et al. 2011) and that abdominal compression alone is superior to leg only compression. As such, 
compression of both the legs and abdomen may be the ideal configuration (Denq et al. 1997; 
Smit et al. 2004). The JSC Cardiovascular and Vision Laboratory developed a 3-piece gradient 
compression garment (GCG) consisting of thigh-high stockings with overlapping biker-style shorts 
that provide 55 mmHg at the ankle decreasing to 35 mmHg at the knee, 18 mmHg at the top of 
the leg, and 16 mmHg over the abdomen. The GCG has zippers over the calf (area of highest 
compression) and along the sides of the biker shorts to aid in donning. The GCG prevented the 
tachycardia and drop in stroke volume during orthostasis normally seen after space flight and 
were comfortable to wear in a group of seven astronauts tested after four different Space Shuttle 
missions (Stenger et al. 2013). 
The GCG also has been evaluated after long duration (~6 months) ISS missions (Lee et al. 
2020c). After 2 minutes of prone rest, crew members were asked to stand as quickly as possible 
and maintain a quiet stand for 3.5 minutes. During this test, the GCG effectively mitigated 
increases in heart rate and decreases in mean arterial pressure when transitioning from the 
prone to upright posture. Overall, the GCG attenuated the postural change in heart rate and 
mean arterial pressure from pre- to post-spaceflight. However, three crew members were unable 
to start the stand test immediately following landing and one other subject terminated to test 
early, likely due to motion sickness. 
f. Current Countermeasures: Cooling 

The Launch and Entry Suit (LES) and Advanced Crew Escape Suit (ACES) that were worn 
by the Space Shuttle astronauts were designed to protect crewmembers against a loss in cabin 
pressure, contaminated atmosphere, and temperature extremes in case of bail out during reentry. Given that the garments were intended to insulate the astronaut from the cold, the LES 
and ACES increased the heat stress to the astronauts during nominal operations. Elevated skin 
and core temperatures cause a shunting of blood to cutaneous vascular beds to dissipate heat, a 
decrease in blood volume due to sweating, and an increase in heart rate (Abraham et al. 1994). 
It was postulated that the increased heat load from the suits contributed to the increased 
incidence of orthostatic intolerance following resumption of Shuttle flights after the Challenger 
accident (Nicogossian et al. 1995). The addition of an in-suit fan that circulated cabin air across 
the upper torso of the astronauts was ineffective (Sawin et al. 1998), and crewmembers 
continued to report heat discomfort. A liquid cooling garment (LCG), a two-piece garment worn 
under the LES and ACES, covers the arms, torso, and legs, and circulates chilled water 
(thermoelectrically cooled using Shuttle power) through a network of small vinyl tubing sewn 
into the fabric, was added to the suit ensemble. In a group of Shuttle astronauts (n=3) who 
inflated anti-G suits and also wore the LCG, heart rate was lower than those who did not wear 
the LCG during re-entry and landing (Perez et al. 2003). Even with liquid cooling, core 
temperature is as much as 0.5 °C higher during re-entry while wearing the re-entry suit than the 
same time of day inflight without the garment (Rimmer et al. 1999). 
g. Current Countermeasures: Pharmacotherapy 
Several different pharmacotherapeutic including fludrocortisone, midodrine, and 
octreotide have been considered for use as countermeasures to post-flight orthostatic 
intolerance. However, none have been found to be suitable due to ineffectiveness in protecting 
against orthostatic challenge, the risk for adverse effects, or the difficulty in administering the 
drug in operational environments. 
Fludrocortisone is a synthetic mineralocorticoid commonly used in Earth-bound patients 
to treat orthostatic intolerance. While administration of a single dose of fludrocortisone helped 
preserve plasma volume in crew returning from short-duration shuttle flights, it failed to protect 
against orthostatic intolerance (Shi et al. 2004). 
Midodrine, an alpha-1 receptor against, also is used to treat orthostatic intolerance 
terrestrially and has been shown to effectively protect subjects from presyncope after two-week 
head-down tilt bed rest (Ramsdell et al. 2001). In a study of a female astronaut with presyncopal 
symptoms after her first spaceflight mission, midodrine administered 1 hour prior to landing on 
her second mission enabled her to complete a post-flight tilt test for the full duration while 
maintaining systolic blood pressure and cardiac output in contrast to her first flight (Platts et al. 
2004). Moreover, midodrine has excellent oral bioavailability and its peak therapeutic effect 
occurs 1 to 2 hours after administration, offering the operational advantage of being able to 
initiate therapy once a decision to land has been made. However, midodrine may cause an 
increased akathisia response when co-administered with promethazine, a common drug used for 
treatment of space motion sickness, due to shared metabolism by cytochrome p450 isozyme 2D6 

(Platts et al. 2006). It also may contribute to QT prolongation, potentially exacerbating known 
effects of spaceflight and drugs such as promethazine. Due to these concerns, midodrine is not 
used as a countermeasure for post-flight orthostatic intolerance. 
Octreotide is a synthetic peptide (octomer) that is an analog of the naturally occurring 
hormone, somatostatin. It is a FDA-approved drug and is used for, among other indications, the 
treatment of hypotension in patients with autonomic dysfunction (Hoeldtke et al. 1991, 1998). 
Octreotide was shown to be superior to midodrine in preventing both post-prandial and 
orthostatic hypotension in 16 patients with autonomic neuropathy (Hoeldtke et al. 1998). A 
double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover study showed that octreotide improved orthostatic 
tolerance to a 45-minute tilt test (Jarvis et al. 2012). However, octreotide must be administered 
as a slow infusion or given subcutaneously to prevent reflex bradycardia and syncope, limiting its 
utility as pre-landing prophylactic countermeasure. 
h. Current Countermeasures: Thigh Cuffs 
Cosmonauts often wear cuffs around the upper thigh, particularly during the early 
adaptation to spaceflight, to reduce symptoms of head congestion, facial puffiness, and 
vestibular disorders (Kozlovskaya et al. 2010). e t i cuffs, called “Braslets”, are composed 
of non-elastic and elastic sections to conform to the upper thigh and are tightened to a level that 
manages symptoms. The cosmonauts learn to adjust the compression of the Braslets in preflight 
training when placed 30°-head-down tilt (Hamilton et al. 2012). Cosmonauts target the 
compression that relieves or reduces symptoms of head congestion and achieves cranial 
impedance rheography waveforms similar to those observed when supine. The Braslets 
nominally apply 30-40 mmHg of compression and are prescribed to be worn for up to 10 
hours/day continuously (Fomina et al. 2004, 2007) or in 2-3 hour segments with 30 minutes of 
rest (Robin et al. 2020). They typically are not worn during exercise or sleep. 
The Braslets were first used in 1984 during a long-duration Mir mission (232 days) and 
were adopted as part of the cosmonaut countermeasure system in 1990. While the application 
of the Braslets decreases central blood volume (lower internal jugular vein diameter, stroke 
volume and cardiac output) and increases fluid distribution to the legs (increased femoral vein 
cross-sectional area) (Herault et al. 2000; Hamilton et al. 2012), their use does not appear to 
prevent signs of orthostatic intolerance during post-flight LBNP tests (Fomina et al. 2004, 2007). 
Similarly, the pilot on the 14-day Gemini 7 mission became presynopal during post-flight 
orthostatic testing despite intermittent use of thigh cuffs during his mission (Stevens et al. 1966). 
Interestingly, Herault et al. reported that the acute effects of Braslet application diminished later 
in the mission (Herault et al. 2000), perhaps due to ill-fitting thigh cuffs due to muscle atrophy 
(Hamilton et al. 2012). 
4. Long-term Health: Cardiovascular Risk Factors 
a. Age 
As of March 2020, 562 astronauts and cosmonauts had flown in space (Smith et al. 2020). 
As of 2021, 350 individuals have been selected to be NASA astronauts, with an additional 10 

selected at the beginning of 2022 (Lifetime Surveillance of Astronaut Health [LSAH], Unpublished 
data). The average age of an individual selected for the astronaut corps is 35 and 33 years for 
men and women, respectively (Hamilton et al. 2005). Men who have flown in space typically 
participated in their first mission in their early 40s and women typically had their first mission in 
their late 30s (Smith et al. 2020). The average age of the active astronauts is currently 52 and 45 
years for men and women, respectively. us, most astronauts are “middle-a ed” by t e time 
they fly in space, and likely to begin to experience age-related health effects during their active 
careers. Whereas spaceflight crews now include more women than ever (women now constitute 
nearly 36% of the current active NASA astronaut corps), the majority of the astronauts and 
cosmonauts who have flown in space are middle-aged men (Harm et al. 2001; Platts et al. 2014; 
Smith et al. 2020), who, because of their age and sex, are more likely to experience dysrhythmias 
and have subclinical manifestations of CVD (Tsao et al. 2022). 
b. Circadian Desynchrony 
Crewmembers sleep significantly less before and during Space Shuttle missions relative 
to their return to Earth (Barger et al. 2014). Adequate sleep is not only essential for cognitive and 
operational performance during EVAs and tasks in spaceflight, but it also is necessary for the 
maintenance of cardiovascular health (Mullington et al. 2009). Variable light-dark cycles, Space 
Motion Sickness (SMS), adaptation to weightlessness and several other factors contribute to 
sleep reduction during space missions. Moreover, adaptations to local time zones immediately 
before and after missions may alter sleep structure and result in changes in circadian rhythm. 
Previous studies have analyzed sleep, performance, circadian rhythms, and light-dark cycles as 
well as pharmaceutical countermeasures during Space Shuttle flights to assess changes in sleep 
quality before, during, and after spaceflight (Putcha et al. 1999; Dijk et al. 2001). It is important 
to evaluate the effects and countermeasures of sleep deprivation during spaceflight not only to 
mitigate errors and accidents but also to consider the implications of CVD, especially for longer 
duration missions to the Moon and Mars. 
Circadian rhythms are physical, mental, and behavioral cyclical changes of natural 
processes that respond to 24-hour light-dark sequences. nvironmental disruptions in one’s 
circadian rhythm, such as artificial lighting, jet lag, or even weightlessness during spaceflight may 
contribute to adverse health implications (Brainard et al. 2015). Myocardial infarction, sudden 
cardiac death, and transient ischemia are all strongly associated with circadian patterns, so there 
is reason to believe that circadian desynchronization may promote risk for cardiovascular events 
(Shaw and Tofler 2009; Thosar et al. 2018). Moreover, shift workers who experience circadian 
misalignment have increased 24-hour blood pressure, inflammatory markers (i.e. IL-6, C-reactive 
protein, and TNF-α , and cardiovascular ris (Mullington et al. 2009; Morris et al. 2016). Animal 
studies with Syrian (Mesocricetus auratus) hamsters have also demonstrated that disruptions in 
normal circadian rhythmicity significantly reduced longevity and increased susceptibility to early 
mortality due to cardiomyopathy (Penev et al. 1998). 
Sleep reduction is associated with spaceflight, and astronauts have reported decreased 
perceived quality of sleep, although objective parameters do not indicate major abnormalities in 

sleep efficiency or structure (Dijk et al. 2001). The average duration of sleep for crewmembers 
during flight was 6-6.5 hours, as compared to the first week post-mission was 7-8 hours (Barger 
et al. 2014). It is important to note, however, some crewmembers may have delayed onset of 
sleep episodes due to increased operational demands or voluntary leisure activities. 
Crewmembers also experienced shorter REM and sleep latencies and higher percentages of REM 
sleep upon return. Case studies on Earth have reported that sleeping less than six hours per night 
significantly increases chances of developing hypertension along with future risks for metabolic 
syndrome (Mullington et al. 2009). Since crewmembers on average receive more than six hours 
of sleep per night and are in peak physical condition, they likely are not as high of risk as short 
sleep duration individuals. 
Ninety-four percent of astronauts have recorded taking some medication during their 
missions, mostly including anti-SMS formulations and sleep aids (Putcha et al. 1999; Barger et al. 
2014). Promethazine was the predominant choice for SMS during the first mission day, but sleep 
medications, frequently zolpidem and flurazepam, were taken throughout the entire mission 
duration (Wu et al. 2018). Zolpidem users have a tendency for decreased risk of heart disease; 
however, flurazepam has been observed to significantly elevate heart rate and inhibit contraction 
of smooth muscle, which may increase risk of heart failure with regular use (Ishii et al. 1983; Kim 
et al. 2018). Both are highly addictive and have a risk for life-threatening sleep behaviors, so 
supervision and trial use before missions is encouraged. 
Melatonin also is used occasionally as a sleep-aid. Dijk et al. (Dijk et al. 2001) observed 
that melatonin (0.3 mg) on alternate nights did not significantly improve sleep on actigraphic 
measurements; however, polysomnography detected that melatonin significantly reduced sleep 
latency. Melatonin has also been found to provide protective effects for some CVDs (Sun et al. 
2016), and adverse arrhythmia side effects are rare for promethazine users, especially for 
infrequent doses. Overall, use of promethazine and sleeping-aid medications under supervision 
by medical professionals for treating SMS and sleep deprivation likely do not pose substantial 
cardiovascular health threats. 
As more crewed missions launch and LEO commercialization gains popularity, an 
increasing number of voyagers will experience desynchronization and subsequently 
cardiovascular stressors. Humans have evolved to respond to 24-hour light-dark sequences, 
 o ever, t e oon’s li t-dark cycle is 29.5 hours. With plans to inhabit the Moon through 
N S ’s rtemis ro ram, it is important to consider that differences in light-dark cycles will 
disrupt astronauts’ circadian r yt ms and may potentiate cardiovascular events ere t e 
environment is much less forgiving. 
c. Traditional Biomarkers: In-Mission 
Whether spaceflight is associated with changes in circulating cholesterol levels and 
proportions is unclear. Lee et al. observed that total cholesterol and LDL increased during 
spaceflight, whereas HDL was unchanged; thus, total cholesterol-to-HDL ratio and LDL-to-HDL 
ratio were elevated (Lee et al. 2020d). Similarly, in the Garett-Bakelman (2019) study of the 

astronaut twin who flew for 340 days on ISS, the ratio of apolipoprotein (APO)-B to APO-A was 
elevated during spaceflight (Garrett-Bakelman et al. 2019). In contrast, Smith et al. observed no 
change in total cholesterol or triglycerides from preflight to postflight (Smith et al. 2005), and 
Hughson et al. observed no change in total, HDL, or LDL cholesterol from preflight to inflight, 
although plasma triglycerides were lower (Hughson et al. 2016). 
Some evidence exists that insulin resistance is increased by spaceflight exposure. Stein et 
al. reported that C-peptide, a metabolite of insulin, was significantly greater than preflight in four 
astronauts after a 9.5-day mission, and they observed similar findings during 12 days of bed rest 
(Stein et al. 1994). More recently, Hughson et al. observed that blood glucose levels were not 
different after long-duration spaceflight but circulating insulin was increased by ~50% and 
HOMA-IR was elevated by almost 60% (Hughson et al. 2016). 
Studies of the carotid artery using ultrasound have yielded inconsistent results across 
three different groups of astronauts. Common carotid artery intima-media thickened in 10 
astronauts within two weeks after launch to the ISS and remained thickened when measured late 
in their 6-month mission and 4 days after landing (Arbeille et al. 2016). In those same astronauts, 
carotid artery stiffness increased and distensibility decreased from pre- to inflight but was not 
different than preflight after landing (Arbeille et al. 2017). In a separate group of eight ISS 
astronauts, carotid artery stiffness increased and distensibility decreased when measurements 
were obtained within 38 hours after landing after a 6-month mission (Hughson et al. 2016). More 
recently, in- and postflight measures of carotid artery stiffness, distensibility, and intima-media 
thickness (cIMT) were not different than preflight in 13 astronauts who participated in ISS 
missions up to 11 months (Lee et al. 2020d). In these studies, there were no reports of incidental 
findings associated with plaque and no cases where cIMT was considered abnormal (>0.9 mm) 
(Mancia et al. 2013) or suggestive of diffuse plaque (>1.5 mm) (Johri et al. 2020). 
Baevsky et al. reported that pulse transit time, another measure of vascular stiffness, 
decreased in cosmonauts after 6-month spaceflights when measured at both three and six days 
after landing (Baevsky et al. 2007a), in agreement with the findings from previous from 
ultrasound measures (Hughson et al. 2016). A more recent report in a separate group of 
cosmonauts did not replicate these findings (Hoffmann et al. 2019), though a wide range of 
postflight responses were reported. 
d. Oxidative Stress and Inflammation: In-Mission 
Several studies have suggested that biomarkers of oxidative stress are elevated during 
spaceflight (Buchheim et al. 2019; Garrett-Bakelman et al. 2019; Lee et al. 2020d), although the 
specific source of the oxidative stress is yet to be determined (Smith et al. 2009). In the course of 
a spaceflight mission, astronauts can experience oxidative stress induced from a variety of 
conditions, including changes in exercise (Moore et al. 2010) and dietary habits (Smith et al. 
2005), exposure to ionizing radiation, psychological stress, and hyperoxic conditions associated 
with extravehicular activity (EVA) and EVA pre-breathe protocols. 

Increased levels of inflammation biomarkers during spaceflight have been observed 
(Crucian et al. 2014; Lee et al. 2020d). Lee et al. ( 2020d) reported that biomarkers of 
inflammation that were increased from pre- to inflight were elevated by flight day 15 and were 
consistently high during the mission. Interestingly, hs-CRP, a biomarker that has predictive value 
in CVD risk (Khera et al. 2018; Gore et al. 2020), decreased for pre- to inflight. Crucian et al. ( 
2014) reported that fewer biomarkers increased from pre- to inflight, although their ability to 
detect changes may have been hampered by the assays’ minimum t res old for detection and 
the statistical treatment. 
Biomarkers of oxidative stress and inflammation that are elevated during spaceflight 
generally return to preflight levels in the days and weeks after landing (Crucian et al. 2014; 
Garrett-Bakelman et al. 2019; Lee et al. 2020d). Notably, hs-CRP and IL-1ra are increased in the 
week after landing, perhaps related to muscle damage (Isaacs et al. 2019) secondary to reloading 
after re-exposure to gravity. The dynamic nature of the readaptation process makes 
interpretation of biomarker data difficult (Lee et al. 2020a); therefore longitudinal surveillance 
of these biomarkers may be more informative. 
e. Traditional Biomarkers: Longitudinal Changes 
Minimal data on CVD biomarkers across the astronaut lifespan has been published. Most 
reports of traditional CVD biomarkers such as cholesterol levels and glucose metabolism 
variables, are only reported inflight and near flight. Recent CVD analyses (Ade et al. 2017; Charvat 
et al. 2022) only include baseline levels of these biomarkers and do not report any longitudinal 
results. Across an individual’s lifespan in the general US population, increasing blood pressure, 
cholesterol levels, and impaired glucose metabolism are noted (Tsao et al. 2022). 
f. Coronary Artery Calcium: Longitudinal Changes 
Coronary Artery Calcium (CAC) scoring measures atherosclerotic burden in the coronary 
arteries using CT scan, with scores computed using the Agatston algorithm ranging from 0 to over 
1000. Higher scores are indicative of a markedly increased risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) 
(4- to 10-fold higher) independent of other risk factors (Radford et al. 2018). Increased CAC scores 
also have been associated with atrial fibrillation and congestive heart failure ( ’Neal et al. 20 5 . 
As astronauts tend to be middle-aged, they already have been exposed to risk factors and 
lifestyle behaviors which along with predispositions due to genetics contribute to accumulation 
of coronary artery calcium. Although CAC scores are considered during astronaut selection and 
selection to spaceflight missions (Hamilton et al. 2006) longitudinal analyses of CAC scores have 
not been published. Inclusion of CAC scores in the Astronaut Cardiovascular Health and Risk 
Modification (AstroCHARM) risk calculator improves the prediction of future cardiovascular 
events in apparently healthy individuals (Khera et al. 2018; Gore et al. 2020). 
While adults who are free of CAC at an initial exam many develop subsequent CAC, the 
utility of serial measurement of CAC in clinical practice is currently being debated in the medical 

literature. Initially it was hypothesized that evaluation of the change in CAC score could enhance 
cardiovascular risk prediction, with an early study from 2004 suggesting that change in CAC may 
be a meanin ful and additive contribution to predictin an individual’s ris (Raggi et al. 2004). 
However, in the last several years population-based studies that have paired scan data and 
adequate numbers of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) events have been reported. 
A large change in CAC score (> 100 U/y) was associated with hard CVD events independent of 
CHD risk factors and baseline CAC score. However, the change in CAC score was only the 5th 
strongest risk factor for CHD, following CAC baseline score, being male, SBP, and total cholesterol 
(Budoff et al. 2013). A report using data from the Cooper Center Longitudinal Study (CCLS) found 
similar results, that while CAC score is associated with asymptomatic CVD, the addition of change 
in CAC scores only modestly improved the prediction of CVD events when compared to a model 
using baseline CAC score (Radford et al. 2018). Most recently, a report from the Heinz Nixdorf 
Recall Study with over 3000 subjects with paired CAC scans over five years and eight years of 
follow-up after both scans found that although various measures of CAC progression were 
independently associated with outcomes, none of the CAC progression algorithms they tested 
demonstrated an improvement in determining risk for hard CHD or hard asymptomatic CVD 
compared with models including risk factors and baseline CAC score (Lehmann et al. 2018). 
5. Long-term Health Consequence: Atrial Dysrhythmias 
 n 20 0, N S convened t e “ trial rr yt mia Summit” and solicited e pert opinion in 
the fields of electrophysiology, cardiology, aerospace medicine, and epidemiology. Panel 
members were given the charge of reviewing cases of atrial dysrhythmias in astronauts with the 
intent of providing recommendations regarding screening, diagnosis, and treatment of the 
astronaut corps, determining lifestyle and space-flight related factors that might predispose 
astronauts to atrial dysrhythmias, and assessing risks to astronauts who participate in longduration spaceflight or extravehicular activities after treatment. At that time, 17 astronauts had 
been diagnosed with an atrial dysrhythmia out of a total cohort of 317 active and retired 
astronauts, representing a prevalence of approximately 5% (Barr 2010; Khine et al. 2018). Most 
cases were asymptomatic, and no occurrences of atrial fibrillation or flutter were recorded during 
flight. Of the cases, 5 individuals presented with atrial dysrhythmias between 2001 and 2010 and 
received radiofrequency ablation treatment. 
While the prevalence of atrial dysrhythmias was not stated as a significant concern, the 
age of presentation was lower in astronauts than in the general population (~40 vs. ~60 years old 
(Abdulla and Nielsen 2009; Barr 2010). The panel suggested two factors that could account for 
earlier than expected presentation of atrial dysrhythmias: more frequent and comprehensive 
screenings in the astronaut corps in the form of annual physicals leading to a higher discovery 
rate and greater levels of vagal tone associated with a high frequency and duration of aerobic 
exercise performed by astronauts (Wilhelm 2014). In middle-aged non-elite distance runners of 
similar age range (40 to 50 years old) lifetime training volume is associated with increased vagal 
tone, prolongation of signal-averaged P-wave duration, and an increase in left atrial volume 
(Wilhelm et al. 2011a). Adaptations such as these, coupled with a higher systolic blood pressure, 

are more pronounced in men than women and may explain the different rates of atrial fibrillation 
associated with sex (Wilhelm et al. 2011b). Other investigators have noted an association 
between exercise volume and risk of atrial fibrillation in men but not in women (Zhu et al. 2016). 
While the Atrial Arrhythmia Summit panel concluded that there was no evidence that 
spaceflight-related factors contribute to the development of atrial dysrhythmias during 
spaceflight, they speculated as to which specific factors might be of concern, particularly for 
future missions: weightlessness-induced cephalad fluid shifts resulting in prolonged atrial 
distension, spaceflight-induced increase in sympathetic activation, plasma volume expansion 
secondary to elevated dietary sodium intake in salt-sensitive individuals due to the use of sodium 
as a food preservative for spaceflight missions, and chronic radiation exposure. 
Continuing to monitor the cardiovascular diagnoses among astronauts, Charvat et al. 
(2021) reported that through 2019, 36 NASA astronauts had been diagnosed with atrial 
fibrillation or flutter for a prevalence of 10%. However, only nine crewmembers were diagnosed 
during their active career. Nine of 36 crewmembers underwent radiofrequency ablation for 
treatment. Compared to a primary care population (Barrios et al. 2012), astronauts had a lower 
prevalence in all age categories except from the ages of 43-49, supporting the pervious 
suggestion that atrial arrhythmias may be diagnosed at a younger age among astronauts. 
6. Long-term Health Consequences: Cardiovascular Disease 
As of the end of 2021, the average age of living retired astronauts is 69 years (range: 39 
to 94 years), with nearly 30 astronauts over the age of 80. Prior to now, few astronauts presented 
with chronic diseases, and analyses for these chronic diseases have been limited to mortality. 
Only recently have enough cardiovascular diagnoses accumulated to begin studying morbidity. 
Interpretations of the data should be viewed with caution considering the low numbers of 
astronauts and the small number of astronaut deaths attributable to CVD. Most analyses have 
been performed to determine the effects of spaceflight on long-term cardiovascular health 
(Appendix A). The majority of these have compared astronaut mortality to other cohorts, 
including the general population, NASA civil servants, and professional athletes, though two 
studies have attempted to assess morbidity. 
The first studies on astronaut mortality were conducted within the Longitudinal Study of 
Astronaut Health (Peterson et al. 1993; Hamm et al. 2000). Peterson et al. examined records from 
195 astronauts selected between 1959 and 1991 compared to the US General population 
(Peterson et al. 1993). Hamm et al. examined the same astronauts compared to a JSC Civil Service 
sample, for a longer follow-up period (Hamm et al. 2000). Cause of death in the astronauts was 
categorized based upon death certificate and autopsy reporting. Peterson et al. found that 
circulatory disease accounted for only two of 20 (10%) reported deaths, and the standardized 
mortality ratios (SMR) in astronauts were 47 (95% CI: 5-168) and 64 (95% CI: 7-230) for circulatory 
disease and ischemic heart disease, respectively (Peterson et al. 1993). The small number of CVD 
deaths in this study yielded large confidence intervals that were not statistically significant. 
Hamm et al. found three of the 195 astronauts and seven of the 575 comparison subjects had 

died from CVD, which resulted in a relative risk of 1.20 (95% CI: 0.27-5.28) (Hamm et al. 2000). 
Accidents accounted for the largest proportion of the deaths in both analyses. Because these 
data ere from early in N S ’s space pro ram, most astronauts ere not at an a e at ic CVD 
would be expected to have developed. 
More recently, astronaut mortality data has been curated from publicly available records 
by Reynolds and Day to compare the astronauts to other groups of individuals. When astronaut 
mortality was compared to the general population of Harris County, Texas (the county in which 
Johnson Space Center is located), astronauts had lower CVD mortality (SMR = 27, 95% CI 9-63) 
(Reynolds and Day 2010). This analysis did not look at the difference in astronauts to the US 
general population for CVD mortality, only all-cause mortality. Another study by Reynolds and 
Day (Reynolds et al. 2014) compared mortality between NASA astronauts and Russian 
cosmonauts. This report found that cosmonauts have a lower risk of death due to any cause 
compared to US astronauts; however, cosmonaut have a higher risk of circulatory disease deaths. 
It is possible, however, that the comparisons between astronauts and cosmonauts are not 
appropriate given that historically cosmonauts have engaged in longer duration missions, 
through the Salyut and Mir Space Station programs, than NASA astronauts and other culturerelated heath behavior differences exist (Shkolnikov et al. 1998). This highlights again the 
potential heterogeneity found within different populations that can contribute to confounding 
factors in epidemiological analyses. 
Reynolds and Day also compared CVD among the Apollo astronauts to address if those 
individuals who flew a mission to the Moon had higher rates of CVD mortality (Reynolds and Day 
2017). Results showed that there was no evidence of increased risk of CVD death in astronauts 
who traveled to the Moon compared to those who did not (SMR = 72, 95% CI: 15-210. There 
continued to be significantly lower CVD death among astronauts compared to the general US 
population (15, 95% CI: 3-43), similar to findings from their other studies. 
A final analysis by Reynolds and Day compared NASA astronauts to professional baseball 
(Major League Baseball) and basketball (National Basketball Association) players (Reynolds and 
Day 2019). Astronauts had lower rates of CVD death than NBA players but could not be compared 
to MLB players on cause specific deaths due to incomplete data. Astronauts also had lower rates 
of death due to any cause than the professional sportsmen. However, similar to other analyses, 
astronauts had higher cause-specific SMR for accidental deaths. 
An analysis performed by Elgart et al. found that among astronauts selected before 1970, 
the SMR for CVD mortality was 33 (95% CI: 14-65) compared to the US white male population 
age and calendar year matched (Elgart et al. 2018). This result was consistent with the findings 
of Reynolds et al. showing astronauts having lower risk of death due to CVD than the general 
population (Reynolds and Day 2010). Additionally, in an internal logistic regression analysis 
comparing astronauts with higher radiation exposure to those with lower radiation exposure, 
there was no evidence that radiation exposure was associated with CVD. Further Elgart et al. 
presented a power analysis detailing the number of astronauts needed per level of radiation 
effectiveness to achieve a power of 80% (Elgart et al. 2018). Sample sizes of over 400,000 deaths 

due to CVD would be necessary to achieve a power of 80% for radiation exposures consistent 
with that of early astronaut cohort. As this number of astronauts will not be likely in the near 
future, this study underscores the need to temper any significant results with an understanding 
of statistical and cohort limitations. 
As stated by Elgart et al., “t e most promisin approac for future radiation research in 
astronaut populations ould be a lon itudinal study it a suitable control population” (Elgart 
et al. 2018). The Lifetime Surveillance of Astronaut Health occupational surveillance program has 
been working to find appropriate cohorts for comparison to NASA astronauts. One promising 
comparison is to the Cooper Center Longitudinal Study (CCLS), a study that began in 1971 with 
nearly 400,000 records from over 100,000 participants. The CCLS is a repository for one of the 
largest and most comprehensive datasets of health-related information in the world collected as 
de-identified clinical data from the Cooper Clinic in Dallas, Texas. The goal is to select comparison 
subjects with more similar baseline characteristics than was possible in previous studies to 
address potential selection bias and healthy worker effects. In addition, larger numbers and more 
detailed medical records are available for this group which should help to control for reporting 
bias. 
A historical cohort study matching CCLS controls to NASA astronauts was undertaken. 
Controls met the cardiovascular qualifying standards (e.g. VO2max > 32.9 ml/kg/min) of the NASA 
astronaut corps and were matched retrospectively on sex, date of birth and age in a ratio of 5:1 
to astronauts who completed at least one spaceflight mission prior to December 31, 2016 
(Charvat et al. 2022). Importantly, this analysis including matching based upon maximal oxygen 
consumption as a measure of overall health because of its relationship to CVD mortality (DeFina 
et al. 2019). Astronauts and matched controls were followed for CVD mortality. A total of 303 
NASA astronauts, alive as of 1970 (the beginning of the CCLS) were matched to 1514 CCLS 
controls (all astronauts had five well matched controls save one who had four). There were 11 
deaths due to CVD among astronauts, and 46 deaths due to CVD among CCLS participants. The 
hazard ratio for the exposure variable of study group was 1.12 (95% CI: 0.57-2.22), indicating a 
slightly elevated, non-statistically significant risk of CVD death among astronauts compared to 
CCLS. When adjusting for baseline cardiovascular covariates of smoking status, cardiorespiratory 
fitness, systolic blood pressure, and total cholesterol, these results did not change (HR: 1.10 [95% 
CI: 0.50-2.45]). 
The low number of CVD deaths in both the astronauts and CCLS participants is indicative 
of two healthy cohorts. These findings have important implications for the assessment of 
astronaut health and suggest that individuals in the astronaut corps are not at an increased risk 
of death due to CVD for the given spaceflight exposures to date. The United States has had over 
20 years of continuous presence in space aboard ISS. The current mission profile for a NASA 
astronaut is six months in LEO; however, missions to Mars will last over three years with no ability 
for return to Earth, and further study of total cardiovascular risk is warranted. 
In one of the few studies of CV morbidity, Ade et al. ( 2017) used data from the LSAH 
Study to compare cardiovascular diagnoses (including death) among astronauts to a matched 

civil servant cohort from Johnson Space Center. In a 3:1 ratio of comparison subjects to 
astronauts, employees were recruited at the time of each astronaut class. Employees were 
matched to astronauts by sex, age, and body mass index. Data were derived from physical 
examinations of active astronauts (required annually), inactive or retired astronauts (voluntary 
participation), and the matched cohort (examinations offered every 1-3 years). Cause of death 
was confirmed by death certificate, with these reports supplemented by autopsy reports in 
astronauts. 
A total of 16 astronauts had a clinical CVD endpoint over a follow-up period of 22 years 
while 46 non-astronaut comparisons developed a CVD endpoint. The incidence rate (per 1000 
person-years) was similar between the two groups with astronauts having 2.34 events and 
comparisons having 2.15 events. There was no difference in the time to CVD event when 
controlling for cardiovascular covariates including age, cholesterol, body mass index (BMI), 
smoking status, and blood pressure. While the LSAH Study provided geographical control for the 
study participants, it had several other limitations. Despite best efforts at matching the cohort to 
the astronauts at baseline, astronauts attained higher educational levels (% graduate degrees) 
and had higher rates of smoking, but lower mean values of blood pressure, glucose, triglycerides, 
and hemoglobin, although these latter differences were likely not clinically relevant. Factors that 
could have influenced these results may have resulted from lifestyle factors; lifestyle data were 
not collected at baseline for most of the subjects and only were included in data collection 
procedures starting in 1994. Also, 11 of the comparison cohort had medical conditions (nine with 
hypertension, two with diabetes) at baseline that would have been disqualifying factors for 
astronauts. However, only one of these 11 comparison subjects died during the study period, and 
analyses excluding these 11 subjects did not substantially affect the outcomes. This analysis also 
did not differentiate mortal and morbid events, only looking at a single cumulative outcome. 
Further, all astronauts regardless of spaceflight experience were included in the analysis limiting 
the interpretation of results to the astronaut occupation. 
In a second aim of the historical cohort study comparing astronauts to a cohort from CCLS, 
cardiovascular morbidity and mortality were studied through 2011 (Charvat et al. 2022). A total 
of 18 CVD events were seen among 300 astronauts and 64 among the 1472 controls. The crude 
incidence of CVD events through 2011 was 1.90 per 1,000 person-years of follow-up for 
astronauts versus 1.37 for CCLS. There was only one non-fatal CVD event among women. NASA 
astronauts had significantly increased risk of CVD events compared to their CCLS matched cohort 
(HR = 1.84 (95% CI 1.04-3.27)). This relationship persisted after adjustment for CVD covariates 
(adjusted HR = 2.41 (95% CI 1.26-4.63)). The etiology for this minor difference in CVD events is 
uncertain. Based on selection criteria, at their time of entrance into the Astronaut Corps, 
astronauts are considered very low risk for CVD. However, during spaceflight astronauts are 
exposed to an environment that induces behaviors that are known to increase risk of CVD, such 
as decreased total physical activity, increased stress, and decreased sleep. 
All analyses of CVD mortality reported thus far have a significant limitation. Most studies 
of astronaut mortality have had higher than expected SMRs for all-cause mortality and accidental 
deaths. In general, accidental deaths have occurred at a younger average age than rates of death 

due to natural causes (Reynolds and Day 2018). Consequently, it is impossible to determine 
whether the individuals who died of accidental causes would have developed CVD had they not 
died at an early age (Austin et al. 2016). One recent study (Reynolds and Day 2018) has looked at 
the possibility of competing risks in astronaut mortality, analyzing differences between natural 
causes of death (e.g., cancer, CVD) and external causes (e.g. accidents). When these competing 
risks are ignored, the assumption of independent censoring is violated, meaning that those who 
die at a younger age are not represented in the population who survive. When these competing 
risks are ignored, the mortality due to a particular cause may be underestimated. Reynolds and 
Day found that this was not necessarily the case among astronauts and cosmonauts (Reynolds 
and Day 2018); however, results should be interpreted with caution as the number of astronauts 
dying from either natural or external causes is low. 
In summary, astronauts have lower rates of CVD mortality compared to the general 
United States populations and similar rates when compared with a well-matched cohort. Factors 
that likely contribute to the lower rates of CVD death among astronauts include extensive 
medical screening that is inherent in the astronaut selection process, the frequent re-evaluation 
of cardiovascular health throughout their career through annual and flight qualification physicals, 
prompt access to treatment should an event arise, and the generally high level of physical fitness 
that the astronauts are expected to maintain throughout their career (Reynolds and Day 2010). 
However, interpretation of the results from these particular studies are impaired by issues 
including lack of control for lifestyle factors (e.g., diet, smoking, alcohol consumption, stress, 
exercise habits) and competing risks of younger deaths due to external causes (Reynolds and Day 
2017). 
7. Long-term Health Consequences: Risk Prediction Algorithms 
NASA flight surgeons currently use risk calculators to estimate risk in astronauts under 
their clinical care. A 2015 analysis by LSAH found differing scores between four different risk 
calculators: an EMR Generated Framingham score, Framingham General CVD risk score, 
Framingham 10-Year Coronary Heart Disease Risk Score and the PROCAM (Prospective 
Cardiovascular Münster study) risk score (Jain et al. 2015). Similar cardiovascular biomarkers 
across the four risk calculators included smoking status, diabetes diagnosis, age and systolic 
blood pressure. While other variables that comprised the risk score differed, a similar ability to 
predict the risk of cardiovascular events among astronauts was observed. 
To improve CVD risk profiling among astronauts, an apparently healthy population, the 
Astronaut Cardiovascular Health and Risk Modification calculator (Astro-CHARM) was developed 
to improve risk prediction using more novel biomarkers (Khera et al. 2018). The American College 
of Cardiology and American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) Pooled Cohort Equation (PCE) was used 
as a baseline model and includes age, sex, race/ethnicity, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, 
systolic blood pressure, use of hypertension medication, current smoking status, and diabetes 
diagnosis. Investigators added coronary artery calcium scoring (CAC), family history of myocardial 
infarction, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), statin use, and glucose levels to see if risk 
prediction could be improved. The addition of CACs, family history and hs-CRP improved the 

ability to predict incident CVD events over the ACC/AHA PCE (Khera et al. 2018). There have been 
no published reports on the use of Astro-CHARM in the general US population. 
In order to improve the long 10 year time frame in which AstroCHARM adequately 
predicts the risk of CVD, investigators sought to create a shorter term (3-year) risk assessment 
for CVD events among adults without known CVD (Gore et al. 2020). This team once again used 
the PCE as a baseline model and tested additional cardiovascular-related biomarkers and imaging 
studies for their relationship to either global CVD or atherosclerotic CVD. Cardiovascular 
biomarkers added included N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), highsensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT) and hs-CRP, and ECG determination of cardiac 
hypertrophy (ECG-LVF). Imaging studies included either a cIMT or CACS, depending on which 
study the data were derived. The only variable found not to be related to either CVD outcome 
was ECG-LVF. The other markers were combined to form a score of 0-4 and a higher score was 
found to be related to increasing CVD risk among the cohort tested. This risk assessment could 
be used for evaluation of healthy individuals, such as astronauts, where short-term assessment 
of risk is needed. However, these findings have not been validated in additional cohorts. 
Minimal data from these risk appraisals for astronauts have been published. After six 
months of spaceflight the estimated 10-year CVD risk was not different than preflight, whether 
calculated with the AHA 2018 Risk Calculator (Grundy et al. 2018) or Astro-CHARM (Khera et al. 
2018), but risk calculated with the AHA 2018 calculator from inflight measures increased a small, 
but statistically significant amount compared to preflight (Lee et al. 2020d). 
B. Ground-based Evidence (Ground Analogs and General Terrestrial Research) 
1. Dysrhythmias 
In general bed rest studies do not report increases in ventricular ectopy, although one 
study has suggested a potential for increased susceptibility based upon microvolt T-wave 
alternans (MTWA) assessed during a graded exercise test. After 9 to 16 days of bed rest, the 
number of subjects who were MTWA positive increased from 17% (4 of 24) before bed rest to 
42% (10 of 24) after bed rest (Grenon et al. 2005); 2 of the 4 subjects who were categorized as 
MTWA positive before bed rest became MTWA negative, while 8 who were negative before bed 
rest were categorized as MTWA positive after bed rest. However, MTWA was not present in any 
subject who had a heart rate below 110 bpm, the threshold for which MTWA would be 
considered clinically significant. Clinically significant MTWA is associated with cardiac arrest, 
sudden death, and ventricular dysrhythmias in patient populations (Rosenbaum et al. 1994; Ikeda 
et al. 2002). 
Multiple bed rest studies have reported decreased cardiac mass, particularly in the left 
ventricle, following chronic exposure to simulated spaceflight (Levine et al. 1997; Perhonen et al. 
2001a; Spaak et al. 2005; Dorfman et al. 2007; Shibata et al. 2010; Hastings et al. 2012; CarrickRanson et al. 2013), with the magnitude of cardiac atrophy associated with the duration of 
exposure (Westby et al. 2016). Additionally, results from a small number of women suggest that 
sex does not affect the magnitude or time course of cardiac atrophy (Tuday and Berkowitz 2007; 

Dorfman et al. 2007; Westby et al. 2016). Cardiac atrophy can be prevented by exercise alone 
(Shibata et al. 2010; Hastings et al. 2012; Carrick-Ranson et al. 2013) or in combination with 
orthostatic-like stress (Dorfman et al. 2007). Although decreases in left ventricular mass would 
be expected to contribute to orthostatic intolerance (Levine et al. 1997) and exercise capacity 
(Dorfman et al. 2007), to our knowledge no bed rest study has yet to report that testing was 
terminated as a result of dysrhythmias (Lee et al. 2010). 
2. Venous Thromboembolism 
Acute changes in blood flow in the arteries and veins in response to spaceflight can be 
modeled using altered gravity simulations such as parabolic flight. Increased IJV diameter and 
pressure are observed during reduced gravity parabolic flight (Martin et al. 2016; Lawley et al. 
2017; Lee et al. 2020b), as has been reported during spaceflight (Arbeille et al. 2001, 2015, 2021; 
Marshall-Goebel et al. 2019). However, stagnant flow does not develop in the IJV upon entry into 
an altered gravity condition from 1-G when subjects are seated upright relative to the change in 
acceleration (Lee et al. 2020b). A research study to investigate IJV flow while subjects are supine 
upon entering weightlessness during parabolic flight to confirm these findings has been initiated. 
Importantly, when seated the flow pattern during reduced gravity parabolic flight is similar to 
that observed when subjects are supine, and stagnant flow does not develop until exposure to 
altered gravity is prolonged (Marshall-Goebel et al. 2019). 
It is unclear whether partial gravity conditions, like those expected on the Moon and 
Mars, will reduce the incidence of stagnant flow compared to weightlessness. There are no data 
available from the Apollo missions that explored this area of venous structure and function. 
Results from a recent parabolic flight campaign suggest that > 0.50-G must be attained before 
restoration of flow similar to that experienced in upright posture in 1-G in a majority of subjects 
(Lee et al. 2020b). Thus, stays in reduced gravity during exploration missions still may include a 
concern for the development of venous thromboembolism. 
3. Orthostatic Intolerance 
a. Bed Rest 
In 2004, slightly over 164,000 patients were hospitalized in the United States with a 
diagnosis of orthostatic hypotension (Shibao et al. 2007). Causes of these hospitalizations ranged 
from simple volume depletion to autonomic failure, with most hospitalizations occurring in those 
over age 65. Previous work has shown that the pattern of post-spaceflight orthostatic intolerance 
is similar to that seen in patients with autonomic failure (Fritsch-Yelle et al. 1996b). However, 
studies that involve ill subjects tend to make extrapolation to the astronaut corps difficult, thus 
a model that includes otherwise healthy individuals is preferable. Bed rest studies, particularly 
those at 6° head-down tilt, in which apparently healthy subjects participate are traditionally used 
as the best ground-based analog to spaceflight (Pavy-Le Traon et al. 2007a; Fortney et al. 2010; 
Lee et al. 2010). Like spaceflight, bed rest results in decreased body mass, reduced blood and 
plasma volume, decreased exercise capacity, and orthostatic intolerance. A recent bed rest study 
replicated the mild hypercapnic environment(ambient CO2 of 0.05%) experienced by astronauts 

on the International Space Station; this exposure did not appear to affect the incidence of 
orthostatic intolerance (Clément et al. 2022b). 
Many bed rest studies have focused on elucidating the mechanisms of orthostatic 
hypotension. These mechanisms include cardiac atrophy, sympathetic dysfunction, and arterial 
and venous dysfunction. Numerous publications have demonstrated cardiac atrophy following 
bed rest (Levine et al. 1997; Iwasaki et al. 2000; Perhonen et al. 2001a), resulting in a stiffer left 
ventricle and decreased stroke volume (Levine et al. 1997). Micro-RNAs associated with 
cardiovascular structure and function were differentially expressed during 30 days of bed rest, 
elucidating pathways that contribute to cardiovascular deconditioning and possible targets for 
intervention (Ade and Bemben 2019). 

Spaceflight-induced plasma volume losses (4-17%) have been replicated using 6° headdown tilt bed rest as an analog (Pavy-Le Traon et al. 2007b). Most of the loss occurs within the 
first week, and plasma volume then remains stable for the duration of bed rest. Recent bed rest 
studies have shown a markedly increased urine excretion upon initiation of bed rest (Trappe et 
al. 2006; Meck et al. 2009). 
Many studies have shown that there is a disruption in the autonomic nervous regulation 
of the cardiovascular system following bed rest. Carotid baroreflex gain is reduced after shortduration bed rest (Convertino et al. 1990; Eckberg and Fritsch 1992) and is associated with an 
increase in orthostatic intolerance. Studies of muscle sympathetic nerve activity (MSNA), a

during the last minute of tilt. This pattern was not seen in the subjects who were able to complete

however, this response was appropriate given the changes in stroke volume and cardiac filling 
pressure and thus reflex control of MSNA was not altered. 
Nitric oxide has been hypothesized to contribute to orthostatic intolerance through its 
effects on the vascular smooth muscle. Bonnin et al. (Bonnin et al. 2001) showed that flowdependent dilation of the brachial artery was increased following seven days of bed rest and that 
this increase was negatively correlated to post-bed rest orthostatic tolerance. There was no 
change in the response to nitroglycerin, implying an endothelium-dependent effect. Bleeker et 
al. (Bleeker et al. 2004) conducted a similar study in the femoral artery following horizontal bed 
rest. They found augmented arterial dilation in response to flow and nitroglycerin, implying an 
endothelium independent mechanism, likely an increased sensitivity to nitric oxide in the 
vascular smooth muscle. It is known that different vascular beds respond differently to the same 
stimuli, which may explain these differences. 
b. Hypovolemia 
Hypovolemia also has been employed as a laboratory model of post-spaceflight 
orthostatic intolerance, specifically using pharmaceuticals to induce a plasma volume loss similar 
to that experienced in astronauts. Kimmerly and Shoemaker used three days of spironolactone 
administration to induce a 15.5 ±1.7% decrease in plasma volume (Kimmerly and Shoemaker 
2002, 2003). While this model was useful for their purposes, spironolactone is known to have 
vasomotor effects, complicating interpretation of studies involving integrated cardiovascular 
responses. Fu et al. used a single dose of Lasix®, which decreased plasma volume by ~13% to 
study the effects of acute hypovolemia on orthostatic tolerance (Fu et al. 2005). Their results 
showed that orthostatic tolerance, as induced by LBNP, was markedly decreased in women, but 
not men, during hypovolemia; but they did not find any differences in norepinephrine responses 
between genders or between normovolemia and hypovolemia. Iwasaki et al. also used a single 
dose of Lasix® and found that the effects on cardiac filling pressures, stroke volume and highfrequency baroreflex sensitivity were similar between hypovolemia and two weeks of head-down 
tilt bed rest; however, they also found that vasomotor function differed between the two 
protocols (Iwasaki et al. 2000). Finally, Meck et al. used a single Lasix® (furosemide) infusion (0.5 
mg/kg) followed by 36 hours of a very low sodium diet (10 mEq/day). This protocol induced a 

plasma volume loss similar to that after spaceflight (Buckey et al. 1996b; Fritsch-Yelle et al. 
1996b; Waters et al. 2002; Meck et al. 2004) (Figure 14). Those who became presyncopal after 
spaceflight and during hypovolemia exhibited the same etiology, a failure to release the extra 
amount of norepinephrine necessary to maintain standing arterial pressure when hypovolemic. 
The differences in these studies may be due to the hypovolemia protocol (acute vs. chronic) or 
in the orthostatic stimulus (tilt vs. LBNP). Regardless of these differences, pharmacologicallyinduced hypovolemia has been shown to reproduce the plasma volume losses seen following 
spaceflight. While obviously useful for some mechanistic studies and countermeasure 
development, this model is limited in that the deconditioning component of spaceflight (and bed 
rest) is not replicated. 
c. Cardiac Mass and Function 
Cardiac muscle is responsive to changes in volume loading conditions. Decreased cardiac 
work during bed rest due to plasma volume loss, reduced end-diastolic volume, and reduced 
physical activity results initially in smaller cardiac volumes and later remodeling to a smaller 
cardiac muscle mass (Westby et al. 2016). Assumption of the head-down position initially 
increases stroke volume with a concurrent decrease in heart rate, with the highest stroke 
volumes achieved ~30 minutes after the start of bed rest. Thereafter, resting stroke volume 
decreases with the lowest value obtained by ~48 hours and remains depressed throughout the 
end of bed rest (Levine et al. 1997). Though head-down bed rest initially results in a cephalic shift 
of ~1 liter of blood from the legs to the upper torso, this is counteracted by humoral responses 
leading to diuresis and attainment of hemostasis at a level approximately half way between 
supine and upright about 24 to 48 hours after the start of bed rest (Levine et al. 1997). 
After two weeks of 6° head-down tilt bed rest, cardiac mass and left ventricular enddiastolic volume measured by 2-d echocardiography was decreased by 5% (p<0.10) and 7% 
(p<0.05), respectively (Levine et al. 1997). This was concomitant with a 24% decrease in LBNP

atrophy (Westby et al. 2016) (Figure 15). Left ventricular mass is reduced in men by 4.7, 8.0, and

Similarly, 60 days of 6° head-down bed rest resulted in decreased left ventricular mass of 9.5% in 
women (Dorfman et al. 2007). Reduced ventricular volume precedes cardiac muscle atrophy; left 
ventricular mass-volume ratio is initially reduced, followed by cardiac remodeling in response to 
decreased wall stress (Perhonen et al. 2001a). For example, left ventricular end-diastolic volume 
decreased by 2 weeks of bed rest, but left ventricular mass was not reduced until 6 weeks. Similar 
results have been obtained from measurements of the right ventricle, although the changes 
ventricular volume and mass are not as great (Perhonen et al. 2001a; Dorfman et al. 2007). 
The decrease in cardiac mass is concomitant with a reduced stroke volume at any given 
filling pressure. In the supine resting condition, after two weeks of bed rest pulmonary capillary 
wedge pressure (PCWP) and left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) are decreased by 18% 
and 7%, respectively (Levine et al. 1997). Starling Curves, constructed from PCWP and stroke 
volume measures during LBNP and with rapid warm saline infusion, indicated a change in the 
cardiac distensibility, particularly at lower filling pressures. Pre- to post-bed rest, PCWP was not 
changed during -15 mmHg and -30 mmHg of LBNP, yet stroke volume was reduced. Surprisingly, 
the relation between LVEDV and stroke volume was unchanged, suggesting that this duration of 
bed rest had no effect on cardiac contractility (same stroke volume per LVEDV); lower stroke 
volume after bed rest resulted from a decrease in diastolic volume. Thus, there is a leftward but 
parallel shift in the filling pressure-volume relation after bed rest. In total, bed rest-induced 
changes in cardiac distensibility leads to lower LVEDV and reduced stroke volume at filling 
pressures representative of low levels of orthostatic stress. The decreased distensibility is 
reflected in a decrease in left ventricular untwisting during diastole (Dorfman et al. 2008).

That these changes in diastolic cardiac performance result from cardiac remodeling,

d. Autonomic Control

Most studies examining HRV in bed rest have found a decrease in HF-RR during bed rest 
or immediately afterwards (Hughson et al. 1994b; Sigaudo et al. 1998; Hirayanagi et al. 2004b, 
a). This bed rest-induced decrease in HF-RR has additionally been evident in bed rest as short as 
20 hours with a furosemide infusion (Patwardhan et al. 1995), during a controlled breathing 
protocol (Iwasaki et al. 2000, 2004), during LBNP (Pavy-Le Traon et al. 1998), and during 
vasoactive drug infusions (Arzeno et al. 2013). Despite some finding no difference in HF-RR with 
bed rest (Ferretti et al. 2009; Shoemaker et al. 2012), it is well accepted that the HRV results 
indicate diminished vagal modulation after bed rest. The majority of studies that examined 
LF/HF-RR as either an index of sympathovagal balance or an indicator of sympathetic activity, 
found LF/HF-RR increased with bed rest (Hughson et al. 1994b; Patwardhan et al. 1995; Pavy-Le 
Traon et al. 1998; Iwasaki et al. 2000, 2004; Hirayanagi et al. 2004b; Coupé et al. 2011). However, 
this increase in sympathovagal balance is not as explicit as the decrease in vagal modulation, 
since other studies did not find LF/HF-RR to change with bed rest (Goldberger et al. 1994; Sigaudo 
et al. 1998; Shoemaker et al. 2012). It should be noted that (Shoemaker et al. 2012) only studied 
a 24-hour bed rest, such that autonomic changes that become significant after the first 24 hours 
would not be detected. Approximation of sympathetic activity by blood pressure variability has 
yielded incongruent results, with increase (Patwardhan et al. 1995; Tanaka et al. 2013; Arzeno et 
al. 2013), no change (Hirayanagi et al. 2004b; Iwasaki et al. 2004; Ferretti et al. 2009), and 
decrease (Iwasaki et al. 2000; Hirayanagi et al. 2004a) in LF-SBP occurring after bed rests of 
various durations. 
Other measures of HRV do not have established links to autonomic activity but can 
quantify overall variability or complexity. When studied throughout bed rest, these measures 
indicate that the total variability and complexity of heart rate decrease with bed rest. The total 
power in power spectral analysis of R-R interval quantifies the overall variability, and has been 
shown to decrease in bed rests ranging from 28 to 90 days (Hughson et al. 1994b; Pavy-Le Traon 
et al. 1998; Sigaudo et al. 1998; Ferretti et al. 2009). Heart rate complexity cannot be quantified 
by typical spectral analysis techniques such as methods based on the Fast Fourier Transform. 
Instead, coarse grain spectral analysis (Yamamoto and Hughson 1991) can be used to extract the 
harmonic and fractal components of HRV and characterize the complexity of HRV by the 
exponent of the 1/f noise in the signal. This exponent corresponds to the slope of the linear fit of 
the fractal component plotted in a log-power vs. log-frequency scale. The spectral exponent has 
been found to increase with bed rest (Hughson et al. 1994b; Fortrat et al. 2001), indicating a 
steeper power drop-off at higher frequencies and a decrease in heart rate complexity. Heart rate 
complexity has also been shown to decrease with bed rest when measured by approximate 
entropy, which quantifies the predictability of fluctuations in heart rate (Goldberger et al. 1994). 
In summary, most of the studies indicate that bed rest causes autonomic dysfunction by 
diminishing vagal modulation, increasing sympathovagal balance, and decreasing the overall 
variability and complexity of heart rate. The effect of bed rest on the approximation of 
sympathetic activity by blood pressure spectral analysis is not clear due to conflicting results. 
Analysis of the heart rate and blood pressure series can further yield information on 
baroreflex sensitivity. Baroreflex sensitivity has been broadly shown to decrease with bed rest, 

as measured by examining the maximum slope of the baroreflex curve (Convertino et al. 1990), 
beat-to-beat sequences of R-R interval and SBP (Hughson et al. 1994a; Pavy-Le Traon et al. 1998; 
Sigaudo et al. 1998; Hirayanagi et al. 2004b, a; Ferretti et al. 2009; Coupé et al. 2011; Arzeno et 
al. 2013), or the transfer function between the R-R interval and SBP (Iwasaki et al. 2000, 2004; 
Custaud et al. 2002; Hirayanagi et al. 2004a). Iwasaki et al. attributed the decrease in baroreflex 
sensitivity after a 14-day bed rest to hypovolemia, due to a reversal of the baroreflex change 
after plasma volume restoration. However, Arzeno et al. (Arzeno et al. 2013) found that the 
decrease in baroreflex sensitivity after a 60-day bed rest was not due to decreased plasma 
volume. 
The bed rest-induced autonomic and baroreflex dysfunction can contribute to post-bed 
rest orthostatic intolerance, and pre-bed rest indices of autonomic function may help identify 
those at higher risk of post-bed rest orthostatic intolerance. In particular, Convertino et al. 
(Convertino et al. 1990) found presyncopal subjects suffered a larger decrease in baroreflex 
sensitivity during bed rest, and Pavy-Le Traon et al. (Pavy-Le Traon et al. 1998) found presyncopal 
subjects to have a smaller increase in LF/HF-RR during LBNP. 
e. Sex Differences 
As in spaceflight, a very low number of female subjects have been studied during bed rest. 
This is traditionally done to reduce the variability in the data and to eliminate the scheduling 
issues related to the menstrual cycle. The women studied have been found to be more 
susceptible to orthostatic intolerance than men after head-down tilt bed rest (Grenon et al. 2006) 
(Figure 18). While the incidence of orthostatic intolerance has been shown to be higher in women 
than in men (Montgomery et al. 1977; Gotshall et al. 1991; White et al. 1996; Shoemaker et al. 
2001; Waters et al. 2002; Fu et al. 2004), the etiology has not been clearly defined. Women have 
greater heart rate responses than men during mental stress (Collins and Frankenhaeuser 1978), 
standing (Gotshall et al. 1991; Schondorf and Low 1992), infusions of pressor agents (AbdelRahman et al. 1994), and cold pressor tests (Girdler et al. 1993). It also has been shown that 
estrogen replacement therapy in postmenopausal women reduces muscle sympathetic nerve 
activity (Hunt et al. 2001; Vongpatanasin et al. 2001). 

Differences in response to stress can be partially due to a dissemblance in autonomic 
regulation between the sexes. Women are often characterized by attenuated sympathetic 
response and enhanced parasympathetic reactivity, particularly during orthostatic stress 
(Convertino 1998; Shoemaker et al. 2001; Evans et al. 2001; Grenon et al. 2006; Barantke et al. 
2008; Nugent et al. 2011; Cheng et al. 2011). These sex differences in autonomic dominance have 
been observed during tilt and stand tests (Barnett et al. 1999; Shoemaker et al. 2001; Grenon et 
al. 2006; Barantke et al. 2008; Cheng et al. 2011), lower body negative pressure (LBNP) 
(Convertino 1998), autonomic blockade (Evans et al. 2001), and vasoactive drug infusions (Arzeno 
et al. 2013). Autonomic control differences between the sexes are preserved throughout bed 
rest, with women also experiencing a decrease in baroreflex sensitivity within a shorter 
timeframe than men (Arzeno et al. 2013). 
In addition, differences in vascular resistance and reactivity can contribute to the sex 
difference in orthostatic intolerance. Women have smaller increases in vascular resistance than 
men in response to LBNP (Frey and Hoffler 1988; White et al. 1996), standing (Frey et al. 1994), 
cold pressor and facial cooling tests (Kilgour and Carvalho 1994), and mental stress (McAdoo et 
al. 1990). Women also may have lower orthostatic tolerance because of increased splanchnic 
blood flow compared to men (Montgomery et al. 1977; Jarvis et al. 2006) and attenuated 
splanchnic constriction in women in the upright posture (Jarvis et al. 2010). The attenuated 
vascular constriction in women also has been observed as attenuated brachial and femoral 
vasoconstrictor responses to increases in transmural pressure (Lott et al. 2009). There could be 
several factors t at contribute to t e omen’s lo vascular resistance, t e most important of 
being estrogen. Several studies in humans demonstrate an augmentation of endotheliumdependent vasodilation with estrogen (Lieberman et al. 1994; Gilligan et al. 1995; Tagawa et al.

a steeper Frank-Starling relationship. Women have larger decreases in stroke volume in response 
to decrements in cardiac filling pressure compared to men, perhaps due to a smaller and stiffer

The sex difference in propensity to orthostatic intolerance, observed during both 
spaceflight and bed rest, leads to an important consideration for countermeasure development. 
A single countermeasure may not be equally effective for both sexes. For example, midodrine 
was less effective in preventing orthostatic intolerance in women than men following simulated 
microgravity (Grenon et al. 2006). 
f. Current Countermeasures: Exercise 
Weightlessness induces cardiovascular deconditioning including cardiac structural and 
functional changes that result in a decreased stroke volume, which plays a key role in the risk of 
post spaceflight orthostatic intolerance. As such, exercise to mitigate this deconditioning is a 
current countermeasure used by crewmembers during spaceflight missions. Similar 
cardiovascular deconditioning occurs during bed rest as during spaceflight; thus, bed rest 
interventions provide an opportunity to assess the efficacy of countermeasures. Recently, Scott 
et al. (Scott et al. 2018) assessed the cardiovascular impact of sedentary bed rest compared daily 
exercise during 70 days of head-down tilt bed rest. The exercise routine combined submaximal 
and high intensity interval aerobic training and resistance exercise. Both the exercise and 
sedentary groups showed an initial decrease in cardiorespiratory fitness, likely due to the loss of 
plasma volume. However, after day 4 of bed rest the exercise group had no further decrements 
in fitness whereas the sedentary group experienced a progressive decline. Additionally, exercise 
was protected against left ventricular atrophy, while the sedentary group showed significant 
reductions in ventricular mass. Others have also demonstrated the utility of exercise to mitigate 
atrophy and maintain ventricular compliance during bed rest using various exercise modalities, 
durations, and intensities (Shibata et al. 2010; Hastings et al. 2012) However, exercise alone, 
regardless of the protocol specifics, does not prevent orthostatic intolerance after bed rest 
(Shibata et al. 2010). 
g. Current Countermeasures: Fluid Loading 
Fluid loading intended to expand plasma volume before re-entry and landing currently is 
implemented to mitigate the risk of post-spaceflight orthostatic hypotension upon return to a 
gravitational environment. Edgell et al. (Edgell et al. 2018) assessed plasma volume and the 
cardiovascular response to lower body negative pressure (LBNP) after three conditions in a cross53 
 
over design: seated four hours with fluid loading and 28 hours of head-down tilt bed rest with 
and without fluid loading. The fluid loading protocol consisted of 1 g of salt per 125 mL water 
with total of 15 mL/kg of body weight consumed, which is consistent with the standard NASA 
protocol. In the seated posture fluid loading expanded plasma volume by an average of 152 ml. 
After 28 hours of head-down tilt bed rest without fluid loading plasma volume was reduced by 
280 mL, and fluid loading did not significantly recover plasma volume to pre-bed rest levels. 
However, stroke volume index and central venous pressure were lower, and renin and 
angiotensin II levels were higher at rest and during LBNP after bed rest without fluid loading, 
suggesting moderate benefits by fluid loading. Interestingly, there was no difference in heart rate 
and mean arterial pressure after bed rest between subjects who performed fluid loading and 
those who did not at rest or during LBNP up to -40 mmHg. The lack of recovery seen in plasma 
volume may have been due to the ongoing diuresis in response to bed rest (Mauran et al. 1999). 
However, this cannot be ascertained clearly as urine output was not recorded after fluid loading 
(Edgell et al. 2018). 
Conversely, Waters et al. (Waters et al. 2005) showed that fluid loading was effective at 
restoring plasma volume after 12 days of head-down tilt bed rest using the NASA fluid loading 
protocol. In this study, plasma volume decreased by ~200 ml from pre- to post-bed rest, and fluid 
loading restored plasma volume to pre-bed rest levels. The heart rate response to 30° head-up 
tilt after bed rest and fluid loading was augmented compared to pre-bed rest but arterial pressure 
was not different, and there were no incidences of orthostatic hypotension pre- or post-bed rest 
head-up tilt. 
Both studies utilized the NASA fluid loading protocol, which is comprised of salt tablets 
and water to produce an isotonic solution after ingestion. Because some crew members reported 
low palatability and gastrointestinal symptoms, other solutions have been developed and tested. 
Fortney et al. (Fortney et al. 1994) compared the ability of salt tablets and water to expand 
plasma volume in supine subjects to an alternative solution, Astro-Ade developed by the NASA 
Ames Research Center. Plasma volume increased ~2% in both the salt tablets and water and the 
Astro-Ade conditions, peaking at 2.5 hours post fluid loading, and decreased by 6% in subjects 
who did not receive fluids. Symptoms of gastrointestinal distress were reported during and after 
consuming both fluids, though only the salt tablets and water resulted in vomiting in one subject. 
Recently, another isotonic electrolyte solution was developed by the NASA JSC Food Laboratory 
for use on commercial spaceflight vehicles and is being tested for efficacy and palatability in a 
ground-based study. 
Attempts to restore blood and plasma volume status by oral fluid loading at the end of a 
spaceflight (Bungo et al. 1985) do not fully prevent orthostatic intolerance. Even restoring filling 
pressures in the face of decreased cardiac distensibility will not prevent the reduction in stroke 
volume (Levine et al. 1997; Perhonen et al. 2001b). Conversely, maintaining diastolic function 
alone through supine cycle exercise fails to prevent post-bed rest orthostatic intolerance (Shibata 
et al. 2010). Similarly, orthostatic tolerance was maintained in subjects who combined rowing 
and resistive exercise throughout bed rest with two days of fluid loading accomplished through 

fludrocortisone and increased salt intake (Hastings et al. 2012). Thus, a combination of fluid 
loading and exercise are necessary. 
h. Current Countermeasures: Lower Body Compression Garments 
The Russian Kentavr and American anti-gravity suit (AGS; pressurized to ~78 mmHg) 
compression garments were highly effective at preventing orthostatic intolerance during a 15minute tilt test in subjects who were in a hypovolemic state to mimic landing day plasma volume 
(Platts et al. 2009) (Figure 19). Both compression garments reduced the cardiovascular stress of 
tilt testing, suggested by lower heart rates, higher blood pressure, and higher stroke volumes 
compared to the control group. While small differences were seen in the cardiovascular 
responses between the Kentavr and the AGS these were not statistically significant. 
Neither garment is ideal; thus, recent laboratory research at the Johnson Space Center 
has been focused on developing a compression garment for exploration class missions that 
improves upon the beneficial features of the AGS and Kentavr. Because the Kentavr (~30 mmHg) 
was effective at only half the compression applied by the AGS (~75 mmHg), the AGS was 
evaluated at “one clic ”, or only ~2 mm (Unpublished data). In this study, orthostatic 
responses were evaluated in hypovolemic test subjects during control conditions (no garment), 
while utilizing the AGS at the lowest operational setting (~26 mmHg), and while utilizing 
commercially-available thigh-high compression garments that applied an average pressure of 25 
mmHg from the ankle to the top of the leg. Neither the AGS at 26 mmHg nor the thigh-high 
compression garments were as effective as the AGS at ~75 mmHg or Kentavr in preventing signs 
and symptoms of orthostatic intolerance. However, in a separate evaluation of these thigh-high 
stockings compared to the AGS, it was found that the stockings did not increase the metabolic 
cost of walking compared to normal athletic attire, unlike the AGS (Lee et al. 2011). The metabolic

A gradient compression garment (GCG), extending from the feet to the lower torso, was

Furthermore, wearing some form of compression garment for 3 days of post-bed rest recovery

i. Countermeasures: Cooling 
Skin-surface cooling is a current countermeasure used during re-entry to protect against 
orthostatic intolerance after spaceflight. Environmental heat stress, which results from the space 
vehicle cabin environment and the insulating properties of the re-entry suit, exacerbates the 
cardiovascular stress of orthostasis and attenuates the compensatory venoconstriction. 
Terrestrial data suggests that skin cooling improve orthostatic tolerance during head-up tilt (HUT) 
in normothermic (Durand et al. 2004) and hyperthermic states (Wilson et al. 2002) by increasing 
cerebral blood flow velocity, peripheral resistance, and mean arterial pressure. Skin cooling 
mitigated bed-rest induced orthostatic intolerance, assessed by LBNP, although tolerance was 
not restored to pre-bed rest levels (Keller et al. 2011). After 2 week of bed rest, forearm vascular 
resistance was higher and heart rate was lower when the skin was cooled during LBNP. 
j. Countermeasures: Pharmacotherapy 
Current pharmacological treatments for adrenergic failure may have application to 
spaceflight-induced orthostatic intolerance. Indeed, midodrine is FDA approved for orthostatic 
hypotension due to adrenergic failure and is discussed in depth under the countermeasure 
section. Such research done at NASA may also benefit the larger clinical community. Again using 
midodrine as an example, we uncovered a drug interaction between midodrine and 
promethazine that was previously unpublished (Platts et al. 2006). Healthy test subjects who 
received midodrine and promethazine together experienced a higher incidence of akathisia than 
controls or subjects with either drug alone. Anecdotal reports from emergency room physicians 
report similar symptoms in patients with diabetic neuropathy who present with nausea and are 
given promethazine while being treated for hypotension with midodrine. In the future, the 
knowledge of this interaction can help avoid unnecessary patient distress and hospital 
admissions in clinical practice. 
k. Current Countermeasures: Lower Body Negative Pressure 
Development of effective countermeasures to post-flight orthostatic intolerance was a 
high priority for NASA's in-house directed medical research during the Space Shuttle program. 
LBNP was one candidate countermeasure under investigation since the 1960s (Fortney 1991). 
Lower body negative pressure (LBNP) is a means of producing an orthostatic challenge during 
spaceflight and bed rest (Guell et al. 1990, 1992; Charles and Lathers 1994). By encasing the lower 

body in a rigid container, venous return can be modulated by varying the level of vacuum. LBNP 
chamber decompression to 50 mmHg provides a cardiovascular stress similar to standing upright 
by pooling blood in the legs and abdomen (Wolthuis et al. 1974). This can be used to simulate 
standing on Earth or other gravity environments, depending on the magnitude of the LBNP. 
Brief decompression challenges have been used as a gravity-independent test of 
orthostatic tolerance, while longer decompressions, either alone or in combination with salt and 
water ingestion, were used as a countermeasure to restore orthostatic responses in bed-rested 
subjects (Hyatt and West 1977; Fortney 1991). A number of LBNP protocols were effective in 
preventing or reversing orthostatic intolerance after bed rest but were prohibitively long (e.g., 
some requiring several days of application or many repetitions) for use during spaceflight 
(Fortney 1991). Hyatt and West (Hyatt and West 1977) demonstrated that a single application of 
only 4 h of LBNP, in combination with the ingestion of one liter of an isotonic bouillon solution, 
restored plasma volume and orthostatic responses to pre-bed rest levels after one week of bed 
rest. Subsequent studies confirmed these findings and also revealed that a shorter duration (2 h) 
LBNP countermeasure was not effective (Fortney 1991). These findings formed the basis for a 
countermeasure to spaceflight-induced orthostatic intolerance that combined LBNP and oral 
consumption of an isotonic solution tested during the Space Shuttle program. The duration and 
frequency of LBNP required to make it an efficient countermeasure, however, is not operationally 
feasible. 
In a recent ground test, LBNP was studied acutely as a countermeasure to the venous 
congestion that might contribute to the risk of SANS and venous thromboembolism (MarshallGoebel et al. 2021). Low level LBNP (-25 mmHg) reduced internal jugular vein cross-sectional 
area while supine by 48% compared to 93% when seated. Further, internal jugular vein pressure 
while supine decreased 5.5 mmHg with thigh cuffs and was 4.3 mmHg lower when seated than 
when supine. 
l. Current Countermeasures: Veno-constrictive Thigh Cuffs 
Acute application of high pressure (60-80 mmHg) thigh cuffs results shifts blood and fluid 
to the legs and away from the head, decreases in right atrial pressure and cardiac output, and 
can cause presyncope in some subjects (Ebert and Stead 1940; Warren et al. 1945). However, 
these pressures are not well-tolerated for long periods of time. Conversely, low pressures (>30 
mmHg) have no demonstratable benefit (Stevens et al. 1966). Most analog studies have 
employed 30-60 mmHg of compression. 
Application of thigh cuffs during brief periods of head-down tilt and water immersion may 
mitigate plasma volume loss but most studies demonstrate that thigh cuffs are not an effective 
countermeasure to orthostatic intolerance after longer exposures (Vogt 1965; Stevens et al. 
1966). For example, in a cross-over design, eight men participated in two 7-day head-down tilt 
bed rest periods separated by one month, once as a control and once as a countermeasure 
subject. Application of thigh cuffs (30 mmHg) for eight hours a day acutely reduced the internal 
jugular vein cross-sectional area and increased femoral and popliteal cross-sectional area when 

head-down, but there was no difference in post-bed rest orthostatic intolerance between the 
two conditions. Further, application thigh cuffs for 10 hours per day during 5 day of dry 
immersion mitigated the reduction in plasma renin and increase in pro-hormone of brain 
naturetic peptide (NT pro-BNP) during the first day but did not prevent the loss of plasma volume, 
LBNP tolerance, and exercise capacity after dry immersion compared to control subjects (Robin 
et al. 2020). In contrast, post-bed rest tolerance to 75° head-up tilt was not reduced six subjects 
who wore thigh cuffs at 40 mmHg for 10 hours per day during 10 days of bed rest (Yao et al. 2008). 
Before bed rest, all 12 subjects completed 20 minutes of head-up tilt, but after bed rest five of 
the six controls and only one of the thigh cuff countermeasure subjects became presyncopal 
before the end of the test. 
In a recent ground test, thigh cuffs were studied acutely as a countermeasure to the 
venous congestion that might contribute to the risk of SANS and venous thromboembolism 
(Marshall-Goebel et al. 2021). Thigh cuffs tightened to produce 60 mmHg of compression 
reduced internal jugular vein cross-sectional area while supine by 31% compared to 93% when 
seated. Additionally, internal jugular vein pressure while supine decreased 4.8 mmHg with thigh 
cuffs and was 4.3 mmHg lower when seated than when supine. Importantly, subjects rated the 
thigh cuffs as the most comfortable countermeasure (compared to LBNP and impedance 
threshold breathing), and subjects that they could be tolerate the thigh cuffs the longest amount 
of time. 
m. Other Countermeasures: Artificial gravity 
Artificial gravity (AG) via short radius centrifugation (SRC) has been suggested as a multisystem countermeasure to spaceflight deconditioning. While the idea of artificial gravity to 
prevent orthostatic intolerance is not new (White et al. 1966), the ideal combination of AG 
magnitude, frequency and duration needed to prevent cardiovascular deconditioning is yet to be 
determined. In a head-down bed rest study, two hours a day of passive standing was sufficient 
to prevent post-bed rest hypotension, although four hours a day of passive standing was required 
to prevent plasma volume losses (Vernikos 1997). Hastreiter and Young determined that 1.5 Gz 
(at the feet) on a short radius centrifuge was required to evoke calf blood flows that were similar 
to those when the subject was standing (Hastreiter and Young 1997). In a non-human primate 
model, Korolkov et al. showed that SRC AG was successful in preventing extracellular fluid loss 
and orthostatic hypotension resulting from bed rest. Furthermore, they determined that 1.2 Gz, 
three times a week was more effective than higher Gz levels, administered four to five times a 
week (Korolkov et al. 2001). Iwasaki et al. determined that daily 1 hour exposures to 2 Gz (at the 
heart) was sufficient to prevent the adverse effects of 6° head-down tilt bed rest on baroreflex 
function and plasma volume (Iwasaki et al. 2001; Katayama et al. 2004). Finally, combining 
exercise with centrifugation at 0.8, 1.2 and 1.6 Gz (at the feet) was previously shown to be 
effective in maintaining orthostatic tolerance after return from 3 to 28 days of simulated 
microgravity (Vil-Viliams 1994). 
The utility of artificial gravity as a whole-body countermeasure has gained momentum in 
the last several years. Three weeks of daily short radius centrifugation (1 Gz at the heart, 2.5 Gz 

at t e feet via t e N S mes esearc enter’s uman erformance entrifu e improved 
orthostatic tolerance in both ambulatory men and women (Evans et al. 2004; Stenger et al. 2007). 
These Ames studies utilized Gz stress in an oscillatory fashion – 2 minutes with 1 Gz at the feet 
followed by 2 minutes with 2.5 Gz at the feet, repeated for up to 45 minutes a day. At NASA 
Johnson Space Center, a similar study was conducted using a constant 2.5 Gz at the feet for up 
to an hour on 6° head-down tilt bed rested subjects. This two-week study evaluated the 
effectiveness of artificial gravity in eight treatment subjects compared to seven control subjects, 
and showed the utility of artificial gravity in preventing bed rest-induced orthostatic intolerance 
and decrements in aerobic capacity (Stenger et al. 2012). However, this study only enrolled men 
as women were unable to tolerate an hour of centrifugation at the intended study magnitudes 
(Fong et al. 2007). Similar results were shown in 11 men who participated in three different 5day bed rest campaigns: as a control (no countermeasure), with 30 minutes of continuous 
centrifugation, and six sessions of 5-minute bouts of centrifugation. Orthostatic intolerance, 
exercise capacity, and plasma volume were mitigated but not preserved at pre-bed rest levels 
(Linnarsson et al. 2015). A more recent study, which included both male and female subjects, 
demonstrated that daily 30-minute sessions of either continuous or intermittent centrifugation 
producing 1-G at the center of mass and 2-G at the feet was tolerated during 60 days of strict 
head-down tilt bed rest (Kramer et al. 2020). Heart rate increased during daily centrifugation, but 
oxygen uptake was unchanged. However, there was no clear benefit to preserving orthostatic 
intolerance in the countermeasure groups (Clément et al. 2022a). 
A recent partial gravity parabolic flight campaign investigated the effects of different Glevels on the headward fluid shift (Lee et al. 2020b) which has been associated with the loss of 
plasma volume and the decrease in orthostatic tolerance. Using IJV distension as an index of the 
headward fluid shift, all G-loads studied (0.25-, 0.50-, and 0.75-G) resulted in headward fluid 
shifts, suggesting that substantially high G-loads would need to be produced to prevent 
cardiovascular deconditioning. Further, the G-levels on the Moon and Mars by themselves are 
not likely to be protective. 
n. Other: Countermeasures: Lower Body Negative Pressure and Exercise 
In a series of bed rest investigations of increasing durations, a team of investigators 
sought to determine whether the combination of lower body negative pressure and exercise 
(LBNPex) would be an effective countermeasure to bed rest-induced deconditioning, including 
orthostatic intolerance, and by extension an appropriate countermeasure system for space flight 
(Lee et al. 2010). The investigator team hypothesized that LBNPex allow astronauts to perform 
treadmill exercise with axial and ground reaction forces (for protection of bone and muscle) 
similar to or greater than that experienced during walking and running in normal gravity while 
simulating the hydrostatic pressure gradients normally experienced by the cardiovascular 
system. Although most exercise performed during bed rest in the supine position fails to prevent 
orthostatic intolerance (Greenleaf et al. 1989b), LBNPex might preserve plasma volume 
(Greenleaf et al. 1992) and stimulate the cardiovascular reflexes used to promote venous return 
and maintain cerebral circulation. 

In the initial study, 24 male subjects completed 5 days of 6° head-down tilt bed rest, with 
subjects divided into three groups. Control subjects performed no exercise during bed rest while 
the remaining subjects performed daily intense bouts of either exercise upright on a standard 
treadmill or supine treadmill exercise within the LBNPex device (Lee et al. 1997). The equivalent 
of one-body weight of loading was achieved by manipulating the chamber opening and the level 
of LBNP (Lee et al. 1997; Ashari et al. 2021). The exercise protocol used by both countermeasure 
groups consisted of 30 minutes of interval-style exercise with prescribed intensities up to 90% of 
pre-bed rest peak oxygen consumption. The exercise protocol previously had been shown to 
maintain peak oxygen consumption (Greenleaf et al. 1989a) and plasma volume (Greenleaf et al. 
1992) during 30 day of bed rest when subjects exercised twice per day on a supine ergometer 
without LBNP. At the completion of the exercise protocol during this 5-day bed rest, subjects 
remained upright or with their legs positioned against the treadmill belt while LBNP was applied 
(Watenpaugh et al. 2007). Before and immediately after 5 days of bed rest all subjects 
participated in head-up tilt test for 30 minutes or until presyncope. After bed rest, tilt tolerance 
time decreased by ~18% in the control group while tolerance time tended to increase in the 
LBNPex group (~9%) (Watenpaugh et al. 1994, 2007). 
Orthostatic intolerance, as assessed by cumulative stress index calculated from total time 
and negative pressures achieved during a LBNP ramp protocol, was not prevented in a 
subsequent 15-day bed rest study (Schneider et al. 2002). Seven men participated once as a 
control and once as a LBNPex subject. Although the sub-tolerance cardiovascular responses to 
LBNP were maintained in the LBNPex condition and not in the control condition, orthostatic 
tolerance was decreased in both conditions (LBNPex: -41%, control: -36%). There were two 
potentially important differences in the countermeasure protocol that may have influenced 
these results. First, the intensity of the exercise was reduced, but the duration lengthened, to 
accommodate the limitations on prescribed exercise intensities that was applied by NASA at the 
time for astronauts (Moore et al. 2010). The peak prescribed exercise intensity during this bed 
rest was 80% of pre-bed rest peak oxygen consumptions and the protocol duration was extended 
from 30 to 40 minutes. Second, the subjects in this bed rest study were not exposed to the 5minute post-exercise LBNP as in the 5-d bed rest study. This additional orthostatic challenge, 
when the subjects were vasodilated from the previous exercise and thermal stress, may have 
been a key component to the success of the countermeasure in the prior study (Watenpaugh et 
al. 2007). 
Subsequent 30-day bed rest studies in identical twins (16 men, 14 women) confirmed the 
potential efficacy of the LBNPex countermeasure against space flight-induced orthostatic 
intolerance (Watenpaugh et al. 2007). One twin was randomly assigned to the control group, and 
one was assigned to the LBNPex group. Control subjects performed no countermeasures 
throughout the duration of bed rest, while the LBNPex subjects performed the same exercise 
prescription as in the previous 15-day bed rest (Schneider et al. 2002) but with 5 minutes of postexercise LBNP as was applied in the 5-day bed rest (Watenpaugh et al. 1994). Pre- to post-bed 
rest tolerance to a combined tilt and LBNP ramp test was not fully protected in the LBNPex group, 
but the 13% decrease was almost 1/3 of the decrease in orthostatic tolerance seen in control 

subjects (-34%). Similar to previous observations in this series of studies, the cardiovascular 
responses to the sub-tolerance tilt and LBNP were maintained from pre- to post-bed rest in the 
LBNPex group but were exaggerated in the control group. These positive results following a 
longer duration bed rest study suggest that the repeated bouts of the post-exercise orthostatic 
challenge is an important aspect of the countermeasure effectiveness (Lightfoot et al. 1989), 
Most recently, the combination of the LBNP and exercise countermeasure was tested in 
women during 60 days of bed rest (Guinet et al. 2009). This bed rest study was different from 
previous ones in this series of investigations in several important ways. First, the number of 
LBNPex sessions was reduced from 6 days per week to only 3 days per week. A resistive exercise 
protocol previously shown to maintain muscle strength and mass (Shackelford et al. 2004) also 
was performed 3 days per week, alternating days with the aerobic exercise sessions so that the 
two countermeasures were never performed on the same day. Second, although the exercise 
portion of the LBNPex protocol was the same as previous studies (Watenpaugh et al. 2000; 
Schneider et al. 2009), the duration of the post-exercise LBNP was extended to 10 minutes. It was 
hypothesized that a greater orthostatic challenge might be necessary to protect against bed restinduced orthostatic challenge after this longer duration bed rest (Guinet et al. 2009), particularly 
in women who are be more susceptible to space flight-induced orthostatic intolerance (Waters 
et al. 2002). Despite this modification, the countermeasures performed during bed rest only 
partially protected against orthostatic intolerance. Tolerance, measured by time to presyncope 
during a combined tilt and LBNP protocol, was reduced by 35% in the LBNPex subjects but by 
50% in this control subjects. Similar to previous studies in this series, sub-tolerance 
cardiovascular responses to the orthostatic challenge were less in the LBNPex group than in the 
control group (Guinet et al. 2009). It was likely that the partial protection against bed-rest 
induced orthostatic intolerance resulted primarily from the LBNP and exercise protocol since the 
resistive exercise protocol alone is not effective after 90 d of bed rest (Belin de Chantemele et al. 
2004), but the contributions of the respective countermeasures was not specifically elucidated. 
In conclusion, the results from this series of studies suggest that LBNP during and after 
exercise is a potential countermeasure for protection against post-bed rest, and by extension, 
post-space flight orthostatic tolerance. Mechanisms for the protection against post-bed rest 
orthostatic tolerance after bed rest might include from protection of plasma volume (Lee et al. 
1997; Watenpaugh et al. 2000; Pavy-Le Traon et al. 2007b) and left ventricular mass and function 
(Levine et al. 1997; Dorfman et al. 2007). The exclusion of the post-exercise LBNP in the one study 
in which post-bed rest orthostatic tolerance and sub-tolerance cardiovascular responses were 
not maintained suggests the importance of the post-exercise orthostatic challenge. Further 
refinement of the exercise and post-exercise protocols may elucidate the respective roles of the 
different portions of the LBNPex countermeasure and result in a more efficacious 
countermeasure for longer duration bed rest and space flight missions. It should be noted that 
in these studies it was occasionally necessary to interrupt post-exercise LBNP exposure due to 
presyncopal symptoms. This occurred more frequently at the start of bed rest and more 
frequently in women (Watenpaugh et al. 2007; Guinet et al. 2009). 
4. Cardiovascular Disease from Radiation Exposure 

The association between high doses of radiation exposure and cardiovascular damage is 
well established. Patients who have undergone radiotherapy for primary cancers of the head and 
neck and mediastinal regions have shown an increased risk of heart and vascular damage and 
long-term development of radiation-induced heart disease. These data are well-detailed in 
several reports (NCRP 2006, p. 2006, 2014, p. 2014; McMillan et al. 2010; Stewart et al. 2012) 
The major driving evidence that proves a link between ionizing radiation exposure and 
the development of degenerative heart and vasculature changes comes from prospective studies 
that follow the long-term, radiotherapy-related effects in cancer survivors. These patients 
received relatively high therapeutic doses (~5–50 Gy) of low-LET thoracic radiation during 
t erapy for cancers of t e ead, nec , and mediastinal re ions, suc as od in’s lymp oma and 
breast cancer (Prosnitz et al. 2005; Darby et al. 2005, 2013; Swerdlow et al. 2007; Carver et al. 
2007; Mulrooney et al. 2009). There is a dose-dependent increase in the development of a wide 
variety of CVDs, including acute and chronic pericarditis, coronary artery disease, 
cardiomyopathy, valvular disease, and conduction abnormalities in these individuals. The study 
of Darby et al. reported the relationship of the risk of ischemic heart disease after breast cancer 
radiotherapy to radiation dose to the heart and to other cardiac risk factors (Darby et al. 2013).

At moderate doses of radiation exposure, between 0.5 and 5 Gy, other evidence that 
supports a link between the occurrence of CVD and radiation exposure is derived from 
prospective studies of Japanese atomic bomb (A-bomb) survivors who received moderate doses 
of radiation (0–2 Gy), as well as from occupationally exposed workers who received continuous 
low-dose exposure (Hayashi et al. 2003; Preston et al. 2003; Yamada et al. 2004; Darby et al. 
2005; McGale and Darby 2008; Shimizu et al. 2010; Gillies et al. 2017; Azizova et al. 2018, 2022; 
Anderson et al. 2021; Oslina et al. 2021). In atomic bomb survivors enrolled in the Life Span Study 
(LSS), the development of health effects has been extensively studied through continuous 
longitudinal health assessments starting in 1958. The average doses that were received by the 
survivors (Preston et al. 2003) are similar to the absorbed doses for an International Space Station 
(ISS) mission (30-120 mGy for six-month stays) and somewhat lower than the absorbed dose 
that is expected for a Mars mission (175-450 mGy ). A significant dose-response relationship 
exists for hypertension, stroke, and heart attack in survivors who were exposed at less than 40 
years of age, indicating an increase in risk with increasing dose. However, the existence of a 
threshold dose could not be excluded for risks associated with the lowest dose category in this 
analysis (Preston 2003) that are associated with doses that are less than 0.25 Sv (Table 1). 
However, cautious interpretation of the patterns of circulatory disease morbidity and mortality 
data is recommended because of the influence of other non-radiation related factors on CVD risk 
and discrepancies in disease classification records (Ozasa et al. 2012) (Wakeford 2022). 
Furthermore, the LSS cohort is comprised of the survivors of the Japanese population who were 
acutely exposed to radiation from a nuclear explosion, which is a drastically different experience 
than that of the astronaut population. Therefore, studies of workers exposed to radiation 
occupationally are of high value for space-related analyses because of the protracted nature of 
exposure to low dose-rate radiation, albeit of a different type. At these moderate doses, 
mechanisms of action are thought to involve atherosclerosis, potentially through inflammation, 
oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, DNA methylation, and cellular apoptosis and 
senescence (McMillan et al. 2010; Bernstein et al. 2020; Meerman et al. 2021). 
For occupationally exposed workers, such as employees of nuclear power facilities, data 
are mixed at this range of moderate doses (0.5-5 Gy). A study of U.S. workers who were exposed 

to chronic low doses of radiation at nuclear power plants showed a significant correlation 
between radiation dose and death from CVD (Howe et al. 2004). However, other occupational 
studies have shown either risks that are more consistent with estimated risks from the atomic 
bomb survivors or no increased risk is observed. The Mayak worker cohort has been subject to 
various recent analyses (Simonetto et al. 2014, 2015; Moseeva et al. 2014; Azizova et al. 2015a, 
b, 2016) and the data from the Mayak workers of a pooled analysis (with the Sellafield workers 
(Azizova et al. 2018) show a highly statistically significant trend in the dose-response relationship 
for ischemic heart disease (IHD) and cerebrovascular disease (CeVD) incidence, albeit with much 
lower and generally non-significant mortality trends for these endpoints. These studies have 
been adjusted for major lifestyle risk factors such as smoking and alcohol use; however, 
interpretation of results is complicated due to internal radiation exposures from internally 
deposited radionuclides such as plutonium, and heavily skewed doses to certain organs like liver 
and lun , predominantly from t ese internal e posures. ternal γ-ray exposures average 0.5-0.6 
Gy, which is moderate in comparison to usually low doses for other occupational-exposure 
studies. The International Nuclear Worker study (INWORKS), comprised of three large national 
groups of workers (for France, UK, US), Gillies (Gillies et al. 2017) demonstrated significant risks 
of circulatory disease, IHD, acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and CeVD. They observed 
significant heterogeneity in all circulatory disease ERR by facility (p=0.006) although not by 
country (p=0.089), however, no such heterogeneity was observed for IHD, either by country or 
facility. A study of US uranium enrichment workers yielded very uncertain estimates of mortality 
ERR/mGy for IHD of 0.019 (95% CI –0.077, 0.26) and for CeVD of –0.13 (95% CI –0.42, 0.44) 
(Anderson et al. 2021). A cohort study of uranium processing workers at the Mallinckrodt plant 
yielded a significant ERR/Gy of 1.3 (95% CI -0.1, 2.8) for IHD mortality (Golden et al. 2022). A 
study of US military personnel exposed to eight atmospheric nuclear tests yielded an insignificant 
ERR/Gy of -0.01 (95% CI -1.2, 1.1) for IHD mortality (Boice et al. 2022a). A cohort of diagnostic 
medical radiation workers in South Korea yielded an all-circulatory morbidity ERR/Gy of 1.4 (95% 
CI -5.7, 9.9), an IHD morbidity ERR/Gy of 12.2 (95% CI -7.1, 47.3). However, the study suffered 
from low statistical power (Little et al. 2021). Finally, follow-up studies of the health risks in 
Chernobyl recovery workers also show excess CeVD and IHD morbidity, but, the cohort had very 
high rates of baseline circulatory disease, which may reflect the elevated rates of incidence and 
mortality from circulatory diseases in the Russian population compared to other developed 
countries (McGale and Darby 2005; Ivanov et al. 2006; Kashcheev et al. 2017). A study of the 
relationship between radiation exposure and atrial fibrillation among atomic bomb survivors did 
not yield a substantive evidence of radiation effects, even after adjusting for sex, city and atrial 
fibrillation risk factors (Haruta et al. 2021). 
In a comprehensive and systematic review by Little et al. (Little et al. 2012), information 
was summarized on circulatory disease risks associated with moderate- and low-level wholebody ionizing radiation exposures with criteria of whole-body radiation exposures and a 
cumulative mean dose of < 0.5 Sv or exposure at a low dose rate (< 10 mSv/day). Although mean

This lack of significance also may be partly due to uncontrolled confounding factors, such as 
lifestyle and genetic factors, within the many studies included in the meta-analyses. Little et al. 
(Little et al. 2021) updated their previous analyses with recent data from moderate and low dose 
studies, and the ERRs they observed remain consistent with the prior analyses (Little et al. 2012; 
Little 2016). 
Molecular epidemiology strategies also are being utilized to supplement the body of 
epidemiological data and provide insight into relevant biological mechanisms and risk estimation 
at low doses (Abend et al. 2015; Kreuzer et al. 2015). 
Currently, a large-scale effort known as Million Person Study (MPS) is underway among 
healthy American workers and veterans to evaluate radiation risks and health effects from low 
dose, low dose-rate exposures. In addition to being a more generalizable population to the 
astronaut corps, this cohort is more suitable to assess dose rate effects than the Japanese atomic 
bomb survivors who were exposed briefly to high-dose radiation in 1945. This epidemiology 
study of US radiation workers and veterans focuses primarily on cancer mortality but will also 
address causes of death due to cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease (Boice et al. 2022c). 
Recent analysis of a major MPS cohort, the worker population at the Los Alamos National 
Laboratory (1943-2017) revealed no evidence of any association between ischemic heart disease 
risks and radiation dose (ERR at 100 weighted-mGy was -0.06 (95% CI -0.16, 0.04; n=3043) (Boice 
et al. 2022b). Increased ischemic heart disease risk from protracted radiation exposure from 
other MPS cohorts is yet to be observed as well. Pooled analysis of MPS cohorts is currently 
underway. 
At low doses <0.5 Gy, the mechanisms of radiation action may involve non-targeted 
effects such as monocyte killing, altered oxygen metabolism and kidney dysfunction. It has been 
hypothesized that radiation may lead to changes in oxygen metabolism, resulting in long lasting 
elevations in reactive oxygen species and other aspects of an inflammatory cascade (Spitz et al. 
2004); there is evidence for long-lasting elevations in vascular reactive oxygen species in the LSS 
(Nakachi et al. 2008). In a 2003 analysis of 992 patients treated for testicular cancer, those who 
received mediastinal radiotherapy showed a significant increase in their risk for a cardiac event 
(Huddart et al. 2003). More notably, patients who received intra-diaphragmatic radiotherapy 
(irradiation below the diaphragm, excluding the heart) still had higher risk compared to controls. 
With a low estimated heart dose of 0.75 Gy, the authors suggested radiation nephropathy, 
leading to hypertension, may be a partial cause for their elevated risk of CVD. Further analyses 
of LSS data by Adams et al. (Adams et al. 2012) and Sera et al. (Sera et al. 2013) show weak 
evidence of kidney as a target tissue and suggest that an increased risk of circulatory disease from 
acute e posures to γ-rays at low to moderate doses (up to 3 Gy) is related to renal dysfunction, 
with concomitant changes in blood pressure and inflammation. This is supported by experimental 
animal data (Lenarczyk et al. 2013, 2020). Adams et al. (Adams et al. 2012) also reported that a 

quadratic dose response model best fits their data, which is consistent with the finding from 
Shimizu et al. (2010) (Shimizu et al. 2010) and Little et al. (Little et al. 2008), who reported that 
inflammation was not increased after low-dose radiation exposure (<0.5 Gy). Tapio et al. (Tapio 
et al. 2021) concluded that pro-atherogenic outcomes like inflammation and premature 
senescence can be observed in heart muscle if endothelial cells or vasculature are exposed to 
low radiation doses even below 0.5 Gy. However, if cells or vasculature are in an inflammatory 
state, low dose radiation exposure functions in an anti-inflammatory manner. 
In summary, these observations demonstrate increased risk for circulatory disease from 
acutely delivered γ-rays and support the existence of a threshold dose, below which significant 
increases in risk cannot be detected. However, it is not known how the threshold might be 
modified for other radiation types, dose-rate effects, or individual sensitivity. For healthy workers 
who refrain from tobacco use and have a normal body mass index (BMI) and moderate alcohol 
use, it remains a possibility that the threshold dose may be higher. In contrast, epidemiological 
meta-studies (Schöllnberger et al. 2012; Little et al. 2012, 2021; Wakeford 2022) are consistent 
with an increased risk below a 0.5 Gy threshold. Additionally, the Health Protection Agency 2010 
report identified several other possible mechanisms, such as mitochondrial dysfunction and DNA 
damage, through which radiation exposure may cause circulatory damage, leaving the existence 
of a threshold an open question. Additionally, the complex nature of high energy particle-induced 
DNA lesions makes uncovering the presence of a threshold dose for space-like radiation a major 
challenge. A recent meta-analysis shows a small but statistically significant increase in CVD 
mortality risk with increasing radiation exposure at doses lower than previously assumed, and 
given the very limited data available, it is not yet possible to quantitatively estimate a specific 
threshold value nor is there data available to resolve whether CVD risks are stochastic or 
deterministic in nature (EPRI Technical Report 2020). 
C. Evidence from Other Organisms (Animal, Cells) 
1. Dysrhythmias 
Ground-based animal studies have determined the effects of simulated weightlessness 
on the cardiovascular system. Tachycardia has been observed in standing rats, after hindlimbunloading for 28 days (Ray et al. 2001). Decreased cardiac mass also has been documented in 
studies of hindlimb-suspended rats (Bao et al. 1999). However, hemodynamics in humans differ 
from hemodynamics in quadrupeds; thus, the rat is not the most appropriate model in which to 
examine weightlessness-induced cardiovascular adaptations (Rowell 1993). 
Recently, mice that were hindlimb suspended for 28-56 days were found to have enlarged 
hearts with decreased contractility as well as an increased susceptibility to pacing-induced 
dysrhythmias as detected by echocardiography (Respress et al. 2014). Further, cardiomyocytes 
harvested from the tail-suspended animals demonstrated an increased leakiness of calcium from 
the sarcoplasmic reticulum, as well as increased frequency of spontaneous calcium release that 
may have been caused by elevated phosphorylation of the ryanodine receptor (RyR2). 
2. Radiation-Induced Cardiovascular Disease 

Systematic studies on the progression of radiation-induced heart diseases were first 
conducted in rabbits (Fajardo and Stewart 1970) and rats (Lauk et al. 1985; Yeung and Hopewell 
1985) with high doses of x-rays in the range of 10 to 20 Gy. Similar studies were conducted using 
heavy ions during the JANUS program at Argonne National Laboratory, in which ultrastructural 
studies of the mouse heart and vasculature were performed after the animals had been 
irradiated with neutrons. The results of the studies were compared with results from irradiating 
mice with low-LET radiation and showed vessel morphological changes, including marked 
fragmentation of vascular smooth muscle layers and an increase in deposition of extracellular 
matrix in vessel walls, similar to the changes observed in animals of advanced age (Yang et al. 
1976, 1978; Stearner et al. 1979). Changes in the coronary arteries and aorta at 18 months postexposure were greater in animals receiving fractionated neutron exposures compared to the 
single-exposure group. Opposite results were observed for the γ-ray-exposed animals, with single 
dose exposures being more effective than fractionated exposures. Relative biological 
effectiveness (RBEs) values for neutron effects increased with decreasing dose or dose 
fractionation, with RBE values exceeding 100 with protracted exposures (Yang et al. 1978). 
Similar results were found in early studies with low doses of neon (Ne) and argon (Ar) ions (Yang 
and Ainsworth 1982). Studies on the atherogenic changes that are associated with irradiation 
were conducted in dogs to compare the effects of fractionated doses of fast neutrons (15 MeV 
avg.) with those of low-LET photons. The RBE of neutrons was estimated at 4 to 5 from these 
studies (Bradley et al. 1981). 
 
 
More recently, studies aimed at defining the mechanisms by which radiation induces 
heart diseases were conducted using atherosclerosis-prone animal models. Increased oxidative

Using wildtype animals, Soucy et al. (Soucy et al. 2011) showed chronic vascular

the galactic cosmic ray (GCR) spectrum. At doses as low as 100 mGy of silicon (Si) ions, changes

with O alone, but only at later time points, unlike earlier observations with Fe ions. Sasi et al. ( 
2017) used a sequential dosing methodology to examined cardiac function in C57BL/6NT mice, 
similar to the AMI surgery group mentioned earlier. No negative effects on cardiac function were 
observed after either three doses of fractionated protons every other day or three fractioned 
doses of protons followed by Fe two days later. Only animals that received Fe prior to three doses 
of protons exhibited depressed left ventricle (LV) function at one month and signs of cardiac 
hypertrophy at three months post radiation. 
Sridharan et al. (2020) exposed male Long Evans rats to whole-body protons and O

effect of Fe was lost when a priming dose of protons was given 24 hours prior to Fe irradiation.

diastolic volume and diameter was observed along with indications of minor cardiac remodeling, 
however, no changes to abdominal aorta was observed. Most recently, Bishawi et al. (Bishawi et 
al. 2022) observed decreased systolic function and increased arterial elastance one year after 1.5 
Gy of GCR5-ion exposure, but not after 0.50 c y 5 Fe, , or 200 c y of γ-radiation in male 
C57BL/6 mice. Changes in aortic elastic fiber organization and alterations in extracellular matrix 
of heart tissue was also observed. 
Miousse et al. observed global DNA hypo-methylation in the 50-UTR LINE-1 14 days after 
exposure to 250 mGy and 1 Gy of O ions, whereas hypermethylation was observed in specific 
LINE-1 elements after 90 days (Miousse et al. 2019). Similarly, Koturbash et al. observed 
hypomethylation of LINE-1 in male C57BL/6J mice one week after exposer to 100 mGy of protons 
or 0.5 Gy of Fe ions (Koturbash et al. 2016). Comparable to Miousse et al. ( 2019), 
hypermethylation of LINE-1, and other retrotransposons as well as major satellite DNA was 

observed 90 days after exposure to Fe ions. Metabolomics analyses revealed involvement of onecarbon metabolism pathway in both the studies. A bioinformatics comparative analysis of 
existing transcriptomic (microarray) data from cardiomyocytes isolated from male C57BL/6 mice 
28 days after 0.9 Gy of protons or 150 mGy of Fe ions and human endothelial cells (HUVECs) 
cultured for seven days on ISS revealed common molecular pathways of oxidative stress with

2019). Interestingly, the transcriptomic signature resulting from exposure to protons also was 
much closer to the signature resulting from signature from spaceflight compared to that resulting 
from exposure to Fe ions. Recently, Garikipati et al. performed transcriptomic analysis on heart

and titanium 22Ti (Ti) ions 16 months after exposure (Garikipati et al. 2021). Analysis revealed 
more profound effects with HZE particles at lower doses than γ-rays. Twelve common

non-linear DEG pattern for Si and Ti ions, contrary to γ-rays. These analyses also revealed no clear 
radiation effect thresholds for lower doses of ions Z or γ-rays. 
 In cellular models, the predominantly studied cell types of cardiovascular system are 
cardiomyocytes and vascular endothelial cells. While Heselich et al. (2018) demonstrated that 
avian cardiomyocytes exposed to Ti, Fe, or carbon (C) ions were radioresistant, Beck et al. (Beck 
et al. 2014a) and Baselet et al. (Baselet et al. 2017) showed that cultured coronary artery-derived 
human endothelial cells were radiosensitive after exposure to 56Fe ions and x-rays and promoted 
distinct pro-atherosclerotic processes dependent on radiation type. Beck et al. showed that 
nickel (Ni) ions irradiation in human umbilical vein cells (HUVECs) at 0.5, 2, or 5 Gy resulted in 
persistent DNA damage, dysregulation of cell cycle, and increased secretion of inflammatory 
cytokines 24 hours after exposure, dysregulation of the cell cycle, and increased secretion of 
inflammatory cytokines (Beck et al. 2014b). Using 3-D micro-vessel models of human endothelial 
cells, Grabham et al. 2011 (2011) and Wuu YR et al. (Wuu et al. 2020) showed that high LET 
charged particles ions inhibit angiogenesis by two distinct mechanisms depending on the LET of 
the particles. 
 In summary, substantial evidence from human epidemiology, animal, and cellular studies 
suggests that low-LET radiation strongly impacts the development of degenerative cardiac effects 
and CVDs at doses above 0.5 Gy, which may be related to the overall acceleration of age-related 
processes. Although recent epidemiological and experimental data points towards a sustained 
risk of CVD below the 0.5 Gy threshold, it open questions include how dose rate and radiation 
quality modify the overall response to radiation exposure. At these low doses, it can be difficult 
to separate effects of radiation from other CVD risk factors, such as smoking status, obesity and 
nutrition, alcohol consumption, and stress. Statistical analyses in human epidemiological studies 
often lack adjustments for these confounders, but newer sophisticated analysis methods are 
improving the confidence in the results from these investigations. These approaches are critical 
to interpreting long-term health effects in astronauts and should include of consideration a 
significant healthy worker effect (Cucinotta et al. 2013a). Unfortunately, ground-based animal 

studies have not provided sufficient data to adequately characterize the risk to the cardiovascular 
system. Some studies have used doses higher than those relevant to spaceflight, lac ed γ-ray 
controls to make estimates of biological effectiveness, and often used animal models that are 
pre-disposed to heart disease that were fed a high-fat diet; thus, the model was not reflective of 
the health status and diet of astronauts. However, it is important to note that observations from 
more recent use of wild-type animals have shown mostly mixed results after radiation exposures 
more relevant to the spaceflight experience. Future work will support better characterization of 
the mechanisms of CVD induction, uncover individual susceptibility, and accelerate the 
development of pharmacological or nutraceutical countermeasures to close knowledge gaps in 
space radiation-induced CVD research. This work will leverage data from multi-ion exposures 
(which is now available with the development of the GCR simulator at NASA Space Radiation 
Laboratory [NSRL]), elucidate sex differences and dose rate effects, identify and utilize better 
animal models, including larger species (e.g., mini-pigs) to improve translation to humans, and 
include newer in-vitro technologies (e.g., tissue-chips, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). 
D. Computer-Based Models and Simulation 
1. Venous Thromboembolism 
Lan et al. (2021) developed a novel model of the cranial circulation, accounting for the 
removal of tissue weight in weightlessness, to explore changes in internal jugular vein flow during 
spaceflight. The initial model was based upon experimental data from their laboratory as well as 
published work from others and considered jugular flow as a single flexible tube (Lan et al. 2021). 
The model was later modified to consider two jugular veins with right-side dominance (Lan et al. 
2022). The model describes a shift in blood flow from the internal jugular veins to the vertebral 
plexus in weightlessness and the lowest flow in the left (non-dominant) internal jugular vein, 
corresponding to the location of a venous thrombosis in an ISS astronaut (Marshall-Goebel et al. 
2019; Auñón-Chancellor et al. 2020). Left internal jugular vein flow decreased from 3.3 to 0.5 
mL/min and right-side flow decreased from 7.4 to 1.2 mL/min when transitioning from 1-G supine 
to weightlessness. The model, however, did not consider the reduction in blood volume during 
spaceflight and is yet to be validated with spaceflight data. 
2. Cardiovascular Disease 
Cucinotta et al. developed a cardiovascular risk model using the same basic model 
structure previously used for cancer (Cucinotta et al. 2013b). A linear no-threshold dose 
response based on the meta-study of Little et al. (2012) was used. Due to a lack of experimental 
and human data, a quality factor based on endpoints that were not CVD specific was used, and 
no dose rate effect modification was assumed since the meta study used occupational and other 
low dose rate cohorts. ICRP and other recommendation for exposure limits for radiation 
protection assume a dose response threshold at 0.5 Gy or Gy-eq whereas model of Cucinotta et 
al. does not assume this threshold. However, there is little direct evidence of a threshold in the 
human data with very few studies showing any evidence of curvature in the dose response that 
would be indicative of threshold-like behavior (Wakeford 2022). While some studies showed a 
statistically significant response above that threshold but not below, this type of behavior is to 

be expected even in the case of a linear dose response as the signal becomes smaller than the 
statistical fluctuations in the data. A critical issue in developing risk models is the shape of the 
cardiovascular dose response. To address this question, Schöllnberger et al. developed a low LET 
risk model using the LSS data based on multi-model inference (Schöllnberger et al. 2018). This 
model includes the possibility of different shaped dose responses, including the possibility of a 
threshold dose, to represent the uncertainty in the dose response. This allows a probabilistic 
estimate of the risk even in the case where there is some probability of threshold. Current 
analysis of experimental data to model quality effects is ongoing but has not yet been published. 
As stated above, the risk of CVD is also highly individualized, dependent on factors such as age, 
cholesterol levels, and calcium score. Recent clinical guidelines (Arnett et al. 2019) directly uses 
CVD risk estimates and part of that decision making process. Huff et al. lays out a modeling 
framework to begin to account for these individual factors in radiation risk assessment (Huff et 
al. 2022). Such an approach can better determine risk both for informed consent before 
spaceflight as well as to help inform medical management decisions related to primary 
prevention of CVD. Recent clinical guidelines (Arnett et al. 2019) directly uses CVD risk estimates 
along with the guidelines suggested by Huff et al. (Huff et al. 2022) to include a framework for 
space radiation risks. 
As pointed out in NCRP commentary 24 (NCRP 2015), biologically-based dose response 
(BBDR) models, which rely on a more mechanistic understanding of the underlying biological 
processes, are a way forward to provide more reliable risk projections when extrapolating 
beyond the available epidemiological data. That report was focused on low dose radiation 
exposures, but this issue can be even more important for space radiation since the radiation 
environment is quite different and more complex than for exposure received terrestrially and 
analyses of the astronaut cohort are quite limited by statistical power. Tapio et al. recently 
reviewed progress in this area (Tapio et al. 2021). Significant progress has been made but a 
comprehensive BBDR model for CVD remains to be developed. 
X. RISK IN CONTEXT OF EXPLORATION OPERATIONAL SCENARIOS 
A. Mission-Related Factors Contributing to Risk 
1. Altered Gravity 
Exposure to weightlessness is associated with the development of orthostatic intolerance 
during re-exposure to gravity. While orthostatic intolerance may present in some astronauts 
after landing on Earth, the risk of orthostatic intolerance also may be present during descent to 
and ascent from Lunar or Martian surface. The orientation of the astronauts in the vehicle and 
the acceleration profile (magnitude, duration, and frequency) will have profound effects on the 
risk, as will the provision of countermeasures. 
 There are no data suggesting that astronauts and cosmonauts will experience orthostatic 
intolerance on the surface of the Moon or Mars. There were no reports of orthostatic intolerance 
during the Apollo missions (Pattarini et al. 2020). Additionally, there currently are no data to 
suggest that exposure to partial gravity environments of the Moon and Mars will be protective. 

Measurements of the IJV from partial gravity parabolic flight (Martin et al. 2016; Lee et al. 2020b) 
suggest that the gravity levels on the Moon and Mars will not be sufficient to prevent the 
headward fluid shift and venous congestion that may contribute to the development of a deep 
vein thrombosis or thromboembolism. 
2. Mission Duration 
Even short stays in weightlessness are associated with post-spaceflight orthostatic 
intolerance (Hoffler et al. 1974; Bungo and Johnson 1983; Bungo et al. 1985), and the prevalence 
is increased in longer missions (Meck et al. 2001; Lee et al. 2015). Exploration missions will be 
longer in duration, and it is plausible that even longer duration missions (>6 to 8 months) will 
increase the risk profile. 
Currently, there is no evidence that mission duration contributes to the risk of 
dysrhythmias or deep vein thrombosis during spaceflight. The cardiac chambers and IJVs are 
distended during even brief exposures to weightlessness, and this persists throughout longduration missions (Khine et al. 2018; Marshall-Goebel et al. 2019). 
Analyses conducted thus far have been insufficiently powered to determine an effect of 
mission duration on the risk of CVD or CVD death after exposure to weightlessness (Charvat et 
al. 2022) but increased mission duration will contribute to total radiation dosage, particularly 
beyond LEO. 
3. Distance from Earth 
Distance from Earth is a critical factor affecting the consequences of a cardiovascular 
health-related event. Astronauts and cosmonauts in LEO are in regular communication with 
ground personnel, with ~2-second delay (Martin et al. 2012), and can be returned to Earth in the 
event of a medical emergency. All ISS crew receive training on independently initiating a medical 
emergency response and at least two crewmembers on each ISS increment receive additional 
medical training to enable support of less emergent issues with assistance from ground medical 
personnel (i.e., flight surgeon) through private voice and video communication. Crewmembers 
and flight surgeons regularly participate in scheduled private medical conferences. Exploration 
missions beyond LEO will experience longer delays in video and verbal communication as the 
distance from art increases, necessitatin t e use of “ ust-in-time” or virtual trainin . During 
missions to Mars, the communication delay will be long enough that astronauts must have 
sufficient knowledge, skills, and resources to diagnose and treat medical contingencies 
autonomously. In addition, rapid Earth return will not be possible necessitating that crew be able 
to identify, treat, and stabilize a broader range of medical issues. 
Increasing distance of travel from Earth also will likely constrain overall mass, volume, 
power, and bandwidth resources resulting in decreased medical diagnostic and intervention 
capabilities unless additional resources can be prepositioned. Also, the increased mission 
duration will extend the time across which hardware must function and during which 
medications would have to remain safe and effective (limited shelf life). 

4. Radiation Exposures 
The environment outside of the shield-like atmosphere and magnetosphere of the Earth 
contains several types of radiation. Galactic cosmic rays (GCR) represent an additional particle 
constituent of space radiation and originate from outside our solar system. GCR is comprised of 
mostly highly energetic protons with a small component of high atomic number (Z), high-energy 
(HZE) nuclei. Because HZE nuclei are the components of space radiation that have the highest 
biological effectiveness, they are a large concern for astronaut safety. HZE nuclei produce highly 
ionizing tracks as they pass through matter. In addition, they leave columns of damage at the 
molecular level when they traverse a biological system, damage that is different in severity and 
complexity from the damage from low-linear energy transfer (LET) radiation sources such as γ- 
and X-rays (Durante and Cucinotta 2008). HZE nuclei impart damage through the primary 
energetic particle and secondary delta-ray electrons as well as from fragmentation events that 
produce a spectrum of other energetic nuclei, protons, neutrons, and heavy fragments (Wilson 
et al. 1995). In low Earth orbit, astronauts will be exposed to GCR only in the polar regions of the

Degenerative tissue effects including CVD are documented following exposures to 
terrestrial sources of ionizing radiation (e.g., γ-rays and x-rays). Degenerative conditions of 
concern that potentially could result from exposures to ionizing radiation in the form of GCR or 
SPEs expected during long-duration spaceflight include CVD (ischemic heart disease, 
atherosclerosis), cardiomyopathy, and cerebrovascular and peripheral arterial diseases leading 
to stroke. However, the existence of low-dose thresholds and dose-rate and radiation quality 
effects, as well as mechanisms and major risk pathways, are not well-characterized. Further, the 
epidemiological evidence regarding lower dose rate has changed in recent years showing that 

lower doses can promote CVDs through microvascular damage and inflammation. As additional 
evidence is pointing to lower, space-relevant thresholds for CVD, additional research with cell 
and animal studies is required to quantify the magnitude of this risk, understand 
mechanisms, and determine if additional protection strategies are required. Accumulated 
exposures from multiple missions resulting in doses above 0.5 Gy-Eq to heart tissue should be 
considered elevated above the threshold for increased risk due to lack of evidence to support 
differences between acute, chronic, and fractionated doses.

Currently, no existing biophysical model projects all degenerative risks for the entire 
range of particle types and energies in space. The large RBEs that are found in the few studies 
that have been performed suggest that an organ dose-equivalent based on radiation quality 
factors can be used to make a first approximation for risk estimates; however, the shape of the 
dose-response curve for specific diseases and dose-rate modifiers are unknown. Dose-rate 
modifiers could be higher than those observed for cancer risks because of the possibility of 
threshold effects. Cucinotta et al. presented risk assessments for extended LEO and exploratory 
missions, including the prediction of a combined cancer and circulatory disease risk for a Mars 
mission (Cucinotta et al. 2013b). The authors used the excess relative risk (ERR) results of Little 
et al. (Little et al. 2012) and noted that the inclusion of the circulatory disease risk increased the

5. Medical Care Capabilities 
Current capabilities to diagnose and treat cardiovascular conditions on the ISS include an 
automated external defibrillator, crew medical restraint system to enable providing chest 
compressions in microgravity, and a limited scope of medications and consumables such as 
epinephrine, beta blockers, intravenous fluids, anti-coagulants, and anti-hypertensives (NASA 
2016). Recently, wearables such as Apple watches have been flown and may provide limited 
single-lead cardiac rhythm monitoring capabilities. Onboard ultrasound devices combined with 
real-time ground subject matter expert support enable crew to perform and acquire high-quality, 
diagnostic sonographic images (Sargsyan et al. 2006). For exploration missions, increasing 
distance from Earth and communication delays combined with reduced or absent resupply and 
crew return capabilities will necessitate more robust onboard medical capabilities, including 
medical hardware and consumables as well as resources to enhance crew autonomy to mitigate 
risk. Specifically, management of de novo dysrhythmias may require the ability to perform 
chemical and/or mechanical cardioversion and cardiac monitoring, to provide long-term 
thromboembolic prophylaxis, and to manage complications such as hypotension. Due to 
communications delays, decision support tools and just-in-time training will be needed to aid 
crews in medical decision making and to assist with performance of imaging or other procedures. 
In situ intravenous fluid generation may be required for Mars missions given that mission 
duration may exceed established shelf-life of intravenous fluid launched from Earth and that 
medical system mass/volume constraints are likely to prohibit carrying a significant quantity of 
intravenous fluid. Development of an exploration formulary that includes relevant medications 
for treatment of CVD and that addresses concerns regarding medication safety and effectiveness 
over the duration and exposures relevant to exploration missions will be needed. 
6. Vehicle Design 

Orientation of the astronauts and cosmonauts relative to the acceleration that they will 
experience during the dynamic phases of flight contributes to the risk of orthostatic intolerance. 
Except for crewmembers on the Space Shuttle and the Lunar Excursion Module, astronauts and 
cosmonauts have been reclined or recumbent to improve orthostatic tolerance during dynamic 
phases of flight. Space Shuttle astronauts experienced as much as 1.7-G during re-entry, and the 
Apollo astronauts experience 0.4-to 0.6-G (Pattarini et al. 2020). Apollo astronauts on the Lunar 
Excursion Module were standing during descent to and ascent from the Lunar surface to increase 
their field of vision of the landing site and to reduce weight of the vehicle (i.e., no seats). 
Protection for solar particle events is an important function of the vehicle design. 
Radiation shielding is described in detail below (Section C.5). 
7. Suit Design 
The protective garments designed for wear during launch and landing on the Space 
Shuttle, the Launch and Entry Suit and the Advanced Crew Escape Suit, were intended to be 
protective in the event of a bail-out, particularly over cold water (Bishop et al. 1999, p. 1999). 
Consequently, without liquid or air cooling, the astronaut might be prone to overheating and 
heat injury. Elevated core temperature could increase the incidence of orthostatic intolerance 
during re-entry and landing on a Lunar or Martian surface. Even with liquid cooling, core 
temperature was as much as 0.5 °C higher during re-entry while wearing the re-entry suit than 
the same time of day inflight without the garment (Rimmer et al. 1999). Current evidence 
suggests that thermoregulation at rest (Stahn et al. 2017) and during exercise (Fortney et al. 
1998) is impaired after 6-months of spaceflight. Given that these changes occurred even though 
crewmembers were performing inflight exercise, if countermeasures are not available during an 
exploration mission, it is possible that impaired thermoregulation may be exacerbated and 
tolerance to standing and exercise will be further reduced. 
The extravehicular suit is a closed system in which it is difficult to provide food and fluids 
to the astronauts. Given that the preparation for extravehicular activities includes significant 
logistical support and other resources, historically these excursions are prolonged (4 to 8 hours). 
Thus, astronauts are likely to become dehydrated, further impairing thermoregulation and 
potentially leading to heat injuries. 
8. Ground Support Availability 
Nominally astronauts and cosmonauts returning from ISS are met by ground support and 
medical personnel soon after landing. For crew returning on the Soyuz capsule, ground support 
and medical personnel are transported by helicopter and set-up shelter at the landing site in 
Kazakhstan for crew medical examinations (Lee et al. 2020c). Currently, for SpaceX-NASA 
missions, the crew land and are recovered in the Gulf of Mexico or in the Atlantic Ocean off the 
Eastern coast of Florida, receive initial care including medical examinations onboard the SpaceX 
recovery ship, and, once they are stable, are transported by helicopter to the rally airport in 
Florida. Should cardiovascular complications arise, prompt access to standard medical 
interventions is available. Landing and the safe recovery of crewmembers on SpaceX vehicles 

continues to provide valuable data, experience, and lessons learned that should be applied to 
similarly planned landings in the Orion vehicle. 
In off-nominal conditions, such as a ballistic re-entry or landing at an unsupported landing 
site, crew may not be reached by the ground support and medical personnel for several hours to 
days, in which case the crew must extricate themselves from the capsule after landing. 
Orthostatic intolerance in this context can delay or impair the ability to egress from the capsule 
and participate in survival activities (Pettit 2010). This situation can be challenging in the first 24 
hours after landing, particularly after long-duration missions (Meck et al. 2001; Lee et al. 2015). 
It is currently unclear how the motion of the waves in the case of a water landing will impact 
egress, orthostatic intolerance, and motion sickness. 
Teams in mission control are in constant contact with crews aboard the ISS. There is a ~2second delay in video and audio communications that may complicate the performance of some 
procedures, such as ultrasound. There is breadth of experience in remotely guiding the crew on 
ISS (Hamilton et al. 2011; Martin et al. 2012; Pavela et al. 2022), but plans are being made to 
improve procedures and provide just-in-time training (e.g. computer-based instruction, video 
review of procedures) as communication delays increase with increasing distance from Earth and 
reliance on remote guidance becomes impractical. 
B. Risks Related to Design Reference Mission (DRM) 
1. Short- and long-duration mission in LEO 
The primary in-mission risks are dysrhythmias and venous thromboembolism. There is no 
clear evidence of increased incidence of dysrhythmias, and the incidence of deep vein thrombosis 
or thromboembolism is yet to be defined. Neither dysrhythmia or venous thromboembolism 
have been monitored systematically so asymptomatic cases may have been missed; however, 
with the recent findings of a venous thromboembolism during an International Space Station 
mission, Space Medicine is actively monitoring crewmembers for venous thromboembolism. 
Orthostatic intolerance remains the primary concern for crew health during re-exposure 
to gravity and in the first few days after landing. Countermeasures are employed in these 
astronauts during re-entry and landing, and care is given to limit their activities based upon their 
clinical presentation. The prevalence of orthostatic intolerance appears to increase with mission 
duration, particularly for missions greater than a few months (Meck et al. 2001; Lee et al. 2015). 
The risk of orthostatic intolerance during re-entry is exacerbated when crew are in an upright or 
seated posture, and when adequate cooling is not supplied (Perez et al. 2003). It has become 
common practice to provide isotonic intravenous saline in the hours after landing following a 
long-duration mission, and food and fluid consumption is encouraged as tolerated (Mulavara et 
al. 2018; Lee et al. 2020c). Some astronauts wear lower body compression garments for several 
days after landing to control symptoms of orthostatic intolerance and manage swelling in the 
legs. Although recovery after spaceflight may be increased after longer spaceflight missions (Lee 
et al. 2015), there appear to be no long-term health consequences related to orthostatic 
intolerance after these missions. 

There is no evidence that spaceflights in LEO result in an increased risk of death due to 
CVD but a recent retrospective analysis suggests that the lifetime risk of a cardiovascular event 
is higher in astronauts than in matched control subjects with no spaceflight experience (Charvat 
et al. 2022). Current estimations of radiation exposure for these missions suggest that the risk of 
radiation-related CVD is not increased as a result of missions in LEO but epidemiological data are 
limited. 
2. Short- and Long-Duration Missions to the Moon 
There currently is no evidence that the risk of dysrhythmias and venous 
thromboembolism are increased in missions to the Moon though it is likely that oxidative stress 
and inflammation will be increased during missions beyond LEO concomitant with an increase in 
radiation exposure. Recent data from partial gravity simulated by parabolic flight suggests that 
the headward fluid shift experienced during weightlessness will not be greatly reduced by lunar 
gravity (0.16-G) (Lee et al. 2020b). 
The risk of orthostatic intolerance is not expected to be greater during re-exposure to 
gravity after lunar missions than after missions in LEO but there likely is an in-mission risk of 
orthostatic intolerance during descent to and ascent from the lunar surface if astronauts are 
oriented in the vehicle such that they experience head-to-foot accelerations (Pattarini et al. 
2020). It is unknown what magnitude and duration of acceleration will be tolerated by the 
astronauts during the dynamic phases of flight, but it appears unlikely that stays in the partial 
gravity environment of the Moon will be protective against orthostatic intolerance. 
Based upon experiences of the Apollo astronauts, there is no evidence that short-duration 
missions to the Moon increases the lifetime risk of CVD (Cucinotta et al. 2016; Elgart et al. 2018) 
but the longest stay on the Moon was only three days. Current estimations of radiation exposure 
during longer missions (up to one year) suggest that the risk of exploration-related CVD risk is 
not increased as a result of missions to the Moon. 
3. Missions to Mars and the Martian Surface 
There currently is no evidence that the risks of dysrhythmias and venous 
thromboembolism are increased in these missions to Mars and the Martian surface though it is 
likely that oxidative stress and inflammation will be increased during missions beyond LEO 
concomitant with an increase in radiation exposure. Additionally, the length of the Mars missions 
will be greater than any previous human spaceflight experience. Current data from partial gravity 
simulated by parabolic flight suggests that the headward fluid shift experienced during 
weightlessness will not be greatly reduced by Martian gravity (0.38-G) (Lee et al. 2020b). 
The incidence of post-spaceflight orthostatic intolerance is likely to be higher than after 
missions in LEO and to the Moon because of the duration of the transit to and from Mars. The 
risk of orthostatic hypotension during descent to and ascent from the Martian surface will be 
present and perhaps higher due to the long transit time. While astronauts and cosmonauts have 
completed one-year missions (Grigoriev et al. 1991; Charles and Pietrzyk 2019; Garrett-Bakelman 

et al. 2019), there is no experience up to three years. Further, the acceleration profiles and the 
orientation of the crew relative to the acceleration vectors are not defined. 
Risk to crew health and mission success may be impacted by the availability of medical 
monitoring and intervention capabilities due to size, mass, and power limitations as well as 
complications with early crew return due to orbital mechanics. Medical interventions are further 
complicated by the availability (stowage) and efficacy (shelf-life) of pharmaceuticals and 
communication delays due to the distance from Earth. Crew selection will strongly influence the 
in-mission risk of a cardiovascular event (Gore et al. 2020) and the skillset of the crew to respond 
to a medical emergency. 
The long-term health consequences of extended travel to, stays on, and return from an 
extraterrestrial destination is unknown given the limited human experience, including stays in 
partial gravity. Estimations of radiation exposure during these Martian missions will approach 
and likely exceed the level at which the risk of CVD will increase. 
C. Countermeasures 
1. Inflight Exercise 
During short-duration spaceflight, exercise reduces the heart rate response to standing 
but does not appear to prevent presyncope (Lee et al. 1999). Exercise prevents cardiovascular 
deconditioning (Dorfman et al. 2007; Hastings et al. 2012), but orthostatic intolerance is not 
prevented without the inclusion of fluid loading (Shibata et al. 2010). 
2. Fluid Loading 
Fluid loading was not performed by NASA astronauts until the Space Shuttle program. The 
efficacy of the end-of-mission fluid loading was demonstrated in astronauts returning from Space 
Shuttle missions (Bungo et al. 1985), although the countermeasure may be less effective in 
preventing signs of orthostatic intolerance after longer missions. Spaceflight-induced plasma 
volume loss is one factor contributing to the development of orthostatic intolerance, but other 
cardiovascular factors contributing to blood pressure regulation during orthostatic stressor also 
may be impaired (Tuday et al. 2007; Behnke et al. 2008). 
The efficacy of the fluid loading countermeasure is affected by timing of ingestion relative 
to the time when orthostatic intolerance protection is targeted. In a ground-based analog, 
Fortney et al. (Fortney et al. 1994) demonstrated that fluid retention peaks about 2 to 3 hours 
after ingestion and plasma volume decreases thereafter. To maximize the benefit of this 
countermeasure, crew time must be allotted for this activity. Future work will need to address if 
distributing the fluid ingestion over several hours, as in the Russian protocol, maintains the fluid 
retention peak profile. 
Fluid loading continues to be used routinely by astronauts and cosmonauts returning 
from International Space Station missions and is planned for use on vehicles from commercial 
providers. Stowage has to be allocated for potable water and salt tablets or isotonic drink mixes. 

The need to fluid loading prior to descent to and ascent from the lunar or Mars surface has not 
been investigated. Based upon results from a 2-week bed rest study (Stenger et al. 2014), fluid 
loading may not be required for short-duration missions when lower body compression garments 
are utilized. 
3. Lower Body Compression Garments 
The efficacy of lower body compression garments has been demonstrated after exposure 
to weightlessness (Vil-Viliams et al. 1998; Perez et al. 2003) and spaceflight analogs (Platts et al. 
2009; Stenger et al. 2014). After short-duration exposures (two weeks), compression garments 
are effective even without the implementation of other countermeasures (e.g., exercise, fluid 
loading) (Stenger et al. 2014). Lower body compression garments also appear to be effective after 
short- and long-duration spaceflight (Stenger et al. 2013; Lee et al. 2020c). However, astronauts 
and cosmonauts may not tolerate the upright posture due to motion sickness (Jennings 1998) 
even when wearing compression garments (Lee et al. 2020c). If elastic garments are used for 
longer missions, a further complication arises from the need to custom-build compression 
garments before spaceflight without the insight into anthropometric changes that may occur 
during the mission before the compression garment is needed. The effectiveness of the garments 
will depend upon the appropriate sizing (e.g., adjustments) required to achieve the desired level 
of compression at the end of the mission. 
Stowage must be allocated for the compression garments on any vehicle in which 
astronauts will return to Earth. The need to wear lower body compression garments during 
descent to and ascent from the Lunar or Martian surface has not been demonstrated. If required, 
stowage requirements would increase if different garments are used for different phases of the 
mission (e.g., one garment for Lunar landing and ascent, and one garment for Earth return). 
Lower body compression garments are regularly worn by astronauts and cosmonauts 
during re-entry and in the hours after landing. Some crewmembers will wear lower body 
compression garments for several days after landing to manage symptoms of orthostatic 
intolerance as well as lower leg swelling (Stenger et al. 2014). It is unlikely that compression 
garments will be needed while on the surface of the Moon. Should they be required for use in 
any protective garment (intra- or extravehicular), their integration into the suit ensemble will 
have to be tested. 
4. Lower Body Negative Pressure 
Use of lower body negative pressure for 4 hours, when combined with fluid loading, has 
been shown to temporarily reverse the development of orthostatic intolerance following seven 
days of bed rest (Hyatt and West 1977) and signs of deconditioning during short-duration 
spaceflight (Charles et al. 1999), possibly through the restoration of plasma volume. The protocol 
tested during the Space Shuttle program required four hours of LBNP and was considered too 
complicated and required too much crew time to be used operationally for crews returning from 
space. Additionally, the LBNP protocol would have to be repeated if landing was delayed. 

However, Russian crewmembers regularly participate in LBNP countermeasures at the end of 
their spaceflight missions in preparation for landing. 
LBNP has been shown to acutely decrease IJV cross-sectional and volume (MarshallGoebel et al. 2021; Arbeille et al. 2021) and restore normal flow patterns in some individuals 
(Marshall-Goebel et al. 2019). However, there is not sufficient evidence to demonstrate its 
efficacy as a countermeasure to the development of a venous thromboembolism. 
Cosmonauts routinely participate in LBNP countermeasure sessions using the Russian 
Chibis Suit in the three to four weeks before landing. The Chibis suit consists of a consist of waist 
seal that is positioned at the level of the iliac crest, a hard shell covering the pelvis, two 
corrugated tubes for the legs, with hard shoes. The plantar surfaces of t e cre member’s feet 
are in contact with the insole of the shoes, and the cosmonauts are encouraged to perform knee 
bends and simulate walking to prevent hypotension. A vacuum pump with a control panel is 
attached to the suit, and the maximum decompression that can be applied 60–70 mm Hg, though 
the training protocol does not exceed -45 mmHg (Kozlovskaya et al. 2010). The amount of 
decompression increased is a staged fashion within a LBNP training session, and across days the 
duration of decompression is increased. 
Currently LBNP countermeasure training is monitored by fellow crewmembers and 
remotely by medical specialists using continuous ECG and intermittent brachial blood pressure. 
In addition to the size and power requirements for exploration-class vehicles, there will need to 
be a way to maintain sufficient medical monitoring capabilities by the crew themselves because 
time delays with increased distance from Earth will prevent practical medical monitoring by 
mission control. Presyncopal episodes were reported during Space Shuttle and Skylab missions 
at or near pressures that provide a similar cardiovascular stress to standing (Henry et al. 1977; 
Charles et al. 1999). Depending on the nature of LBNP use during exploration-class missions, the 
duration and frequency will need to be optimized for targeted countermeasures. 
5. Radiation Shielding

from trapped radiation and SPEs. (NCRP 1989, 2006). Health risks associated with protracted 
exposures to the ubiquitous space radiation environment poses as one of the most critical 
challenges facing planners of long duration exploration class missions and possibly a future 
sustained habitat in space. These health risks range from risk of cancer, degenerative diseases 
such as diseases of circulatory system, central nervous system, cataract, and immune system 
risks, to acute radiation syndromes. There are huge uncertainties in estimating these risks, mainly 
due to large uncertainties in the radiobiology of heavy particles.

6. Radiation Countermeasures 
Traditional compound-based approaches to reduce radiation health impacts include 
either administering radioprotectors, medical countermeasures administered prior to a known 
radiation exposure that counter the immediate radiation damage or radiation mitigators 
following radiation exposure, targeting molecular processes that cause damage, or enhancing 
pathways that promote repair to minimize the damage associated with radiation exposure. 
Much of the indirect cell damage produced by ionizing radiation is mediated through 
reactive oxygen species (ROS) that are immediately generated from the interaction of the 
charged particle with water and other cell components (Li et al. 2014). Excessive production of 
free radicals produces oxidative damage to cellular structures, which includes proteins, DNA, and 
lipids, and contributes to the radiation-induced degenerative changes that are associated with 
aging, CVD, and cataract formation. This extensive ROS profile also can induce persistent 
metabolic changes that result in a chronic inflammatory response. The subsequent recruitment 
of inflammatory cells, such as macrophages, which also generate ROS and pro-inflammatory 
signals, feeds into a loop of oxidative stress, inflammation, and cell damage (Zhao and Robbins 
2009). 
Two main types of countermeasures have been used to protect vasculature from ionizing 
radiation: sulfhydryl or thiol compounds and antioxidants. Both classes of compounds function 
by scavenging the free radicals produced by the interaction of ionizing radiation with water. 
WR2721, known as Amifostine/Gammaphos/Ethyol, is the best-described member of the 
sulfhydryl class and is the only drug that is approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) 
to help prevent excess damage to normal tissues during radiotherapy. The mechanism of action 
of this drug is thought to be the scavenging of free radicals produced by radiation and H-atom

et al. 2012). Small animal studies with GT3 showed radio-protective effects on the intestine 
potentially by accelerating intestinal immune cell recovery (Garg et al. 2019). GT3 was shown to 
attenuate radiation-induced cytogenetic damage in human umbilical vein endothelial cells

and dose rates experienced in space, the use of anti-inflammatory drugs for the prevention of 
radiation-induced effects also is a possibility. Wilson et al. (Wilson et al. 2011) used corticosteroid 
therapy to improve the conditions of radiation-induced pneumonitis and pneumonopathy, but it 
should be approached with caution due to evidence pointing to immune dysregulation in 
weightlessness (Crucian et al. 2018). The use of a long-term anti-inflammatory drug may 
synergize with the depressed immune function to significantly increase the risk of infection and 
even CVD (Hsu and Katelaris 2009). 
Another potential avenue for biological countermeasures is the use of statins, a class of 
pharmaceuticals currently used to slow or prevent CVD. A recent study has utilized simvastatin 
to mitigate the effects of radiation-induced CVD (Lenarczyk et al. 2015), albeit after very high 
doses of whole-body irradiation (10 Gy γ- rays). Aside from biological countermeasures, aerobic 
training has also been suggested as a safe approach for potential mitigation of CVD after 
radiotherapy or chemotherapy (Berkman and Lakoski 2015; Yu and Jones 2015). In general, 
suggested cardioprotective strategies for radiation-induced CVD include early and frequent 
monitoring of cardiovascular health through modern approaches like clonal hematopoiesis of 
indeterminate potential (CHIP) mutations and other CVD biomarker monitoring, non-invasive 
imaging techniques (e.g., electrocardiography, echocardiography), management of traditional 
risk factors (e.g., hypertension and dyslipidemia) through diet, exercise, and pharmaceuticals, 
and surgical intervention as required (Cutter et al. 2013). 
Identifying or developing an effective medical countermeasure that addresses multiple 
indications and provides cross-risk mitigation by targeting common pathways for each health 

impact is desirable but challenging. This becomes even more important due to the gaps in 
knowledge regarding the cardiovascular system response to space-relevant radiation types, 
doses, and dose rates. Undoubtedly, the recently upgraded GCR simulator at NSRL, that provides 
a more accurate ground-based representation of the space radiation environment, will play a 
critical role in evaluation of effective medical countermeasures to mitigate space radiation 
effects (Sishc et al. 2022). 
XI. DIRECTED ACYCLIC GRAPH REVIEW AND INTEGRATION WITH OTHER RISKS 
 
 
A. Directed Acyclic Graph Narrative 
Altered gravity and radiation are considered the two main hazards contributing to this 
risk (Figure 22). Altered gravity results in a headward fluid shift, which sets off a cascade of events 
that include plasma volume loss, decreased red cell mass, cardiac atrophy, and vascular 
adaptations. These are modified by individual factors (e.g., preflight fitness, existing medical 
conditions), performance of exercise countermeasures, dietary practices (i.e., available food 
choices and personal preferences), and circadian rhythm disturbances. Changes in blood flow 
during spaceflight may contribute to skeletal muscle atrophy, bone demineralization, and 
decreased exercise capacity and tolerance. Orthostatic intolerance during re-exposure to gravity 
results from physiological adaptations combined with human integration factors, including 
vehicle design (e.g., body orientation relative to acceleration, cabin environment heat load) and

suit design (e.g., insulating properties of the garment, provision of cooling) as well as the use of 
countermeasures (e.g., lower body compression garment, end-of-mission fluid loading). 
Decreased or loss of consciousness or symptoms associated with reduced cerebral brain 
perfusion can impact crew activities (e.g., piloting, egress) and contribute to the decreased 
capacity or failure to complete mission objectives or critical tasks. 
Should a medical condition arise during a spaceflight mission, successful treatment is 
reliant upon the skill set of the crew (i.e., prior training, access to just-in-time training), 
communication capabilities with medical support personnel (i.e., audio and visual 
communication, data transfer between ground teams and vehicle), communication delays (that 
increase with increasing distance from Earth), medical monitoring capabilities (e.g., 
electrocardiogram, ultrasound or other imaging, biological sample analysis, radiation exposure 
monitoring), medical treatment capabilities (e.g., pharmaceutical or nutraceutical agents, 
defibrillation or cardioversion, cardiopulmonary resuscitation), and the ability to return the 
crewmember to Earth or abort the mission for more extreme circumstances. 
Radiation exposure results in a long-term health risk, influenced by the amount and type 
of radiation and individual factors (i.e., non-modifiable [family history/genetics, age, sex] and 
modifiable [diet, exercise, smoking habit] risk factors). Radiation exposures result in the 
generation of reactive oxidate species and inflammation that contribute to the development of 
many chronic diseases, including CVD. Primary and secondary tissue damage lead to cardiac and 
vascular dysfunction and disease. Long-term health outcomes for the astronauts are dependent 
upon recognition of symptoms, appropriate occupational surveillance activities, routine medical 
monitoring, and access to medical personnel and facilities for diagnosis and treatment. 
B. Integration with Other Risks 
Risk of Reduced Physical Performance Capabilities Due to Reduced Aerobic Capacity 
• Cardiovascular structure and function will have a direct impact on aerobic exercise capacity. 
Cardiovascular deconditioning is associated with lower maximal and submaximal exercise 
tolerance (Lee et al. 2010). 
• Countermeasures to prevent aerobic capacity deconditioning also will be protective of 
cardiovascular structure and function (Dorfman et al. 2007, 2008; Hastings et al. 2012) and 
may control modifiable risk factors associated with the long-term health of the astronauts 
after spaceflight.

Risk of Impaired Performance Due to Reduced Muscle Size, Strength, and Endurance 

• Changes in skeletal muscle performance during unloading are preceded by vascular 
adaptations (Trinity et al. 2022). Alterations in blood flow and distribution to skeletal muscle 
may contribute to reduced exercise tolerance. 
• Change in muscle size and volume, particularly during long-duration spaceflight (Thornton et 
al. 1977), will alter the level of compression provided by elastic lower body compression 
garments, unless the sizing of the garment is adjustable and can be changed by the astronaut 
during the mission to accommodate changes in anthropometry. 
Risk of Ineffective or Toxic Medications During Long-Duration Exploration Spaceflight 
• Medications for cardiovascular interventions must have sufficient shelf-life and be resistant 
to radiation exposure for the appropriate treatment of expected cardiovascular 
complications during an extended spaceflight mission (e.g., three-year round-trip to Mars). If 
unanticipated cardiovascular events occur, the appropriate medications may not be 
available, and thus crew health and performance may be compromised. 
Risk of Performance Decrement and Crew Illness Due to Inadequate Food and Nutrition 
• Insufficient fluid intake during spaceflight could exacerbate the impaired thermoregulation 
associated with spaceflight (Fortney et al. 1998) and spaceflight analogs (Lee et al. 2002), 
leading to reduced exercise tolerance and the potential for heat injury. 
• Insufficient fluid intake (i.e., fluid loading) at the end of the mission also may contribute to 
orthostatic intolerance. 
• Inappropriate food choices during a mission also might be a contributing factor to 
unfavorable changes in modifiable CVD risk factors (e.g., blood lipids). 
Risk of Spaceflight Associated Neuro-ocular Syndrome 
• The headward fluid shift that occurs in weightlessness is hypothesized to be one of the 
contributors to the development of spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome (SANS). 
Although the exact mechanisms and long-term outcomes are not well defined, one sign of 
SANS is the engorgement of the choroid behind the eye. This might be associated with venous 
congestion, as suggested by changes in IJV dimension and pressure (Marshall-Goebel et al. 
2019). It has been suggested that endothelial dysfunction (Zwart et al. 2017) and changes to 
the glycocalyx (Zwart et al. 2022) contribute to alterations in the blood brain barrier, possibly 
another factor in the development of SANS. 
• Recently, Mao et al. examined potential disruption of the blood retinal barrier in mice 
through monitoring key proteins in the structure and function of the blood-retinal barrier 
after whole body exposure to a mixed energy source (Mao et al. 2022). Indices of increased 
blood-retinal barrier permeability were observed after an exposure that replicates the 
anticipated dosage experienced during a Mars mission (0.5 Gy), suggesting that radiation 
could contribute to ocular and brain changes in spaceflight. Though the animals were not 
hindlimb unloaded, the findings were similar to previous results from spaceflight. 

Risk of Performance Decrements and Adverse Health Outcomes Resulting from Sleep Loss, 
Circadian Desynchronization, and Work Overload 
• Increased psychological stress and circadian misalignment (Guo et al. 2013, 2014) are 
associated oxidative stress, inflammation, and the development of CVD. 
Risk of Adverse Health Event Due to Altered Immune Response 
• Biomarkers during spaceflight are suggestive of a low-level chronic inflammatory state that 
may increase the risk of long-term health consequences, including CVD. 
Risk of Altered Sensorimotor/Vestibular Function Impacting Critical Mission Tasks 
• Sensorimotor disturbances after spaceflight may contribute to cardiovascular responses to 
orthostatic stress and result in symptoms similar to orthostatic intolerance. The combined 
effects could impair safe egress performance. 
Risk of Bone Fracture due to Spaceflight-induced Changes to Bone 
• Changes in bone blood supply may contribute to bone demineralization and impair 
fracture healing.

B. State of Knowledge/Future Work 
Exposure to weightlessness alters blood volume, flow, and pressure distribution, 
manifesting in changes in vascular and cardiac structure and function. Cardiovascular 
deconditioning is a well-recognized consequence of exposure to weightlessness, contributing to 
orthostatic intolerance and decreased exercise capacity. In-mission countermeasures have been 
instituted, and available evidence from four to six-month missions in LEO suggest that functional 
impairments recover in the weeks after landing with the assistance of a reconditioning program. 
Less is known following missions >1 year, and there is minimal experience with missions beyond 
LEO longer than 14 days or in partial gravity longer than 3 days. Epidemiological evidence does 
not support an increased lifetime risk of CVD death in astronauts, but mission durations have 
been limited largely to less than 6 months and there has been limited long term follow-up for 
those crewmembers. However, a recent report suggest that the lifetime risk of cardiovascular 
event is greater in astronauts with at least one spaceflight mission when compared to a wellmatched cohort, though the number of events is low in both populations compared to general 
population (Charvat et al. 2022). 
Terrestrially in humans, a statistical increase in atherosclerosis-related cardiovascular 
pathologies is observed at late time points when exposed to ionizing radiation (e.g., γ-rays and 
x-rays) at and above 500 mGy. 20 N uidance states “t ere is not sufficient evidence t at

limitations will affect medical capability and clinical outcomes in missions, particularly in missions 
at a greater distance from Earth. 
C. Mitigation Strategy 
The strategy for understanding and mitigating spaceflight-induced cardiovascular effects 
is stepwise and includes: 1) Understanding and characterizing of effects of long duration 
weightlessness and radiation – separately and in combination, 2) developing of countermeasures 
by first ground and thereafter low-earth orbit (LEO) tests, and finally 3) testing in the deep space 
(Gateway) environment. 
For characterizing and understanding the effects of each of the major spaceflight 
environmental factors (weightlessness, radiation, isolation, confinement, and stress) in isolation 
and in combination, ground-based analogs using in vitro methods, animal models, and human 
subjects (parabolic flight) will be used, as well as long-duration flights of cells/animals/humans in 
LEO. Because experimental radiation studies are generally limited to in vitro and animal models, 
the effects of space radiation (thresholds, dose-responses and the effects of chronic or 

protracted dose-rates) in isolation and in combination with other spaceflight stressors will be 
characterized using a multifaceted approach. This approach includes long-term occupational 
surveillance; leveraging data from human radiotherapy patients and previously flown astronauts 
of various durations; tracking of potential new biomarkers of CVD susceptibility such as CHIP 
(clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential) mutations, using/developing animal, cell 
culture, and organ-on-a-chip models for ground-based laboratory studies using space radiation 
analogs (e.g., NSRL). Monitoring of astronauts and rodents in Gateway and on the Lunar surface 
will also be conducted and data will be analyzed by translational and computational modeling. 
Countermeasures to orthostatic intolerance have been used for many years by both NASA 
and the Russian Space Agency. Future work may address verification of the countermeasures in 
an operational setting and refinement of the countermeasures to address commercial crew and 
exploration missions. Current work includes characterization tolerance to G-loads less than 1-G 
in women and men who are hypovolemic, mimicking the plasma volume loss experienced during 
spaceflight. Specifically, this addresses concerns for orthostatic hypotension for the Artemis 
missions and will be used for definition of the acceleration limits (G-load and duration of 
exposure) during descent to and ascent from the Lunar surface. Further, this study intends to 
define whether a lower body compression garment is required during these dynamic phases of 
flight for the Human Landing System. 
Countermeasure development for non-orthostatic intolerance concerns will be stepwise 
in that firstly, data mining, emerging research and developments highlighted at conferences, 
literature review, the NASA process of Request for Information (RFI), studies using human 
induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), and workshops with key experts will constitute the basis 
for selection of the candidates to be tested. Then, tests will be conducted on the ground in 
primarily animal models followed by countermeasure efficacy tests in ISS astronauts. Finally, data 
collection on astronauts during various Gateway and lunar missions using the suggested 
countermeasures will complete the mitigation strategy. 
XIII. SECTION V: CONCLUSIONS 
Although some aspects of cardiovascular system-related risk are well-characterized (e.g., 
orthostatic intolerance) and operational countermeasures are in place (e.g., lower body 
compression garments), evidence regarding other potential concerns is emerging (e.g., venous 
thromboembolism). Continued surveillance of cardiovascular biomarkers and long-term health 
outcomes are required to define the risk to crew health and mission success, particularly during 
and after long exposures to weightlessness and partial gravity as well as space radiation 
exposures. Evidence from ground-based research, terrestrial medicine, and spaceflight analogs 
will continue to be critical. Development of countermeasures during and providing health care 
after the missions is a duty of the national and international space agencies that engage these 
missions, in cooperation with the commercial partners.
